# STÅLHAGEN II · ISVÄGEN

*the ice road* — design document, revision 1 (after critique)

## 0. Pitch, and how to read this

**Pitch.** December 1997, the winter the cooling towers went cold. The bay froze for the first time in the narrator's life, and the machines that had been "cleared away" in 1991 stood up out of the ice. The narrator of *Stålhagen*, now twenty-one and a junior line inspector for a power company that cut the islands off six years ago, is driven out along the shore road with a sled in the back of a van, because the meter at the shore substation runs backwards some nights and somebody has to go and look. What they find is that everything on the islands still answers when you call it, that black cables nobody laid run from the facility across the ice into the village and down into the hole where SV-14's slab used to be, that the small four-legged machines have grown larger siblings with cradles on their backs, and that the thing at the facility which answered the radio and the bell in 1989 has been answering the islanders too. It has only ever had one answer. The game is a 3D action-horror walk in the first game's voice: over the shoulder, one bay, eight places joined by ice, a bearer that comes closer only while you hold still, a cutter that severs where the blue flows, a darkroom where photographs take eight seconds to show what the eye did not, and a rule above every other rule that the player is never rushed. Everything runs from one HTML page; every image, mesh, material and sound is drawn in code.

**How to read this.** Section 1 lists the decisions that are Julian's to make, in the order they constrain everything else; each states what changes in the document if he chooses otherwise. Section 2 is the direction as revised after the panel and the critiques. Sections 3 to 8 are the deep dives (creatures and combat; world and place; narrative and continuity; art, rendering and photography; audio; pacing, controls and accessibility), edited for consistency with each other. Section 9 is the numbers of record: the one table every other section quotes, and the engine layout and build order that implement it. Section 10 is the charter that protects the walk, with its enforcement and tests. Section 11 is the production plan. Section 12 holds the revisions made after critique, the continuity table with the first game, and a glossary of the Swedish. Where a number appears in a section and also in §9, §9 wins; the sections have been edited to agree, and a lint over `levels.js` and `text.js` keeps them agreeing. Spelling is British throughout. Units are metres, seconds and metres per second unless stated. Nothing in this document is marketing; where a thing is a risk, it says so.

## 1. Decisions for Julian

Fourteen decisions, ordered by how much of the rest they constrain. The first three are close to settled and are listed so that they are confirmed rather than assumed. Each gives the question, the options with one line of tradeoff, the recommendation with its reason, and what in this document changes under the alternatives. Forks the deep dives raised that turned out to be settled by the brief (no clock; no faces; sit is absolute; no forced kill; the colour law) are not listed; they are rules now, in §10.

### D1. Engine: raw WebGL2 forked from grift-city and merged with steelhag

- **Raw WebGL2, no dependencies, single-file dist under 3 MB** — owns the look (fog, ink line, the display transform, per-light specular), keeps the repo's zero-dependency line; costs writing skinning, spot shadows and every shader feature yourself (about three weeks a library would give).
- **three.js from a pinned CDN** — skeleton, spot shadows and a grey-box in a week; the height fog, ink line and per-light specular become `onBeforeCompile` surgery, the dist must inline 650 KB, and the chunked-mesh performance model is re-created by hand.
- **WebGPU** — nicest volumetrics; breaks "opens anywhere" on iPad and Safari today.

**Recommendation: raw WebGL2.** The hard parts (creatures, the room graph, colliders, room audio, painted materials, the design) are library-agnostic; the look lives in a fragment shader we own; a hall with forty draws and two shadowed lights is inside what the city already holds at 30 Hz on an iPad. *If three.js:* §9's file layout loses `gl.js`, `render.js`, `skeleton.js` and half of `meshes.js`; §9.3's M2 and M5 shrink by about two weeks; the smear, ink and haze pull are rebuilt as passes; every other section stands.

### D2. Team track and scope

- **Three people, ten months to release** — slice at week 8, core at week 36, release at week 44; the cut order is unlikely to be touched below item 3.
- **Two people, thirteen months** — slice at week 10, core at eleven months; the cut order likely reaches item 4 (HALL 2's line becomes one head turning).
- **One person with an AI pair, seventeen to eighteen months** — slice at week 13, core at month 14; Friday is device day and nothing new starts; the cut order likely reaches item 6; Kyltornen is gone from the start.

**Recommendation: decide by the people available; plan for the two-person track.** The scope in this document (seven chapters, an epilogue, two stretch chapters, about 28,600 lines) is the panel's; the two stretch chapters are the buffer. *If solo:* §11's schedule is the third column and the sites are drafted by the pair with every bench, vantage, line and encounter placed by hand.

### D3. Camera: over the shoulder, the painting camera, the viewfinder; fixed cameras decided per room

- **Over the shoulder with a painting camera and a first-person viewfinder** — keeps the orange figure in the landscape, the body as HUD and the player's own hand in the standoff; costs a swept camera and two tunings of every interior.
- **First person throughout** — cheapest camera, best for the torch and tight halls; loses the small figure against the big machine and the sight of yourself being looked at, which is the horror.
- **Hybrid: fixed composed cameras in some facility rooms** — halls become literal paintings and cost no camera sweep; splits the control scheme and steals intimacy from any standoff indoors.

**Recommendation: over the shoulder, with the fixed camera grey-boxed in the culverts in week one and decided per room, not per building.** The culverts are where the shoulder camera will fail first (a 2.2 m tube against a 0.25 m sweep sphere), so that is where the fixed camera is tried. HALL 1, Mätrummet and any room with a bearer keep the shoulder regardless, because the standoff needs the hand in frame. *If first person:* §3's standoff loses its best evidence at the gait gate; §6's painting camera survives only as the sit shots; the slice is a week cheaper.

### D4. Beds and days: any bed spends a night; light belongs to the chapter card

- **Any bed spends a night; the calendar drifts with your nights; light changes only when a chapter card plays** — the drifting date is the game's quietest record of failure, and a place revisited is seen under the current chapter's fixed light; the nights table is bounded so repeated sleep cannot empty the game.
- **Sleep only at each chapter's end bed** — dates stay fixed and every chapter is seen in its authored light; the nights table collapses to one row per chapter and the carry is the only thing that moves the calendar.

**Recommendation: any bed.** It keeps "you can always sleep when you are ready" true, and the card that reads *December 20th* at a place you should have reached on the 18th is the design's most economical consequence. *If chapter-end beds only:* §4.6's table gains a "fires only on chapter sleep" column; §5.2's computed dates become authored; four beds become the four chapter-end beds and the docked-hull bunk is a sit.

### D5. The standoff's release rule

- **Release is never a flinch; only active inputs are** — the hold can be let go at any time and the hand comes back; the flinch set is sustained movement over 120 ms above a 0.35 deadzone, hurry, torch on, flash, the cutter raised, a flare lit. The nerve test is the twitch at 1.0 s, which exists to make the player jerk the stick. Letting go leaves the bearer's arms a fifth unfolded and its grapples open, so the next hold starts from there and its twitch comes 0.3 s earlier.
- **Release after the twitch is a flinch; before it is harmless** — the twitch becomes a visible point of no return (grapples open, LED steady) and the hold gains a fail state, which the fear critic wanted and the tone and coherence critics called a QTE by the brief's own definition.

**Recommendation: release is never a flinch.** Three of four critics, the accessibility toggle (hold or press) and the touch dead zone all need one rule, and this is the one under which "inaction is always safe" stays true on the pause screen. The tension is carried by the twitch, the wrong-mode chord, the head turning to the hand, and the fact that nothing resets when you let go. *If the point-of-no-return version:* §3.6's "Failing" paragraph gains a second clause and the hold-or-toggle setting is removed for reach out; §8.4's touch dead zone of 80 ms on release becomes a fail condition; the listening test's standoff question stays.

### D6. The vertical-slice scene and chapter IV's light

- **The standoff on the snow field with the window lit behind you belongs to chapter IV's return home, and IV's fixed light is the afterglow (sun 3° below the horizon)** — the card still reads *Sunset at ten to three*; KV-3 stands in the last warm band of sky; the field is dark enough for a lit window and light enough for the gait to be judged; the slice builds IV's light row.
- **The scene opens chapter VI at the hub at night** — the darkest version; VI would then start at the farm rather than at Båten's culvert, and chapter IV's return home would be uneventful.

**Recommendation: chapter IV, afterglow.** Fixed light per chapter forbids "coming home in the dark" in a sunset chapter, and two of three critics chose IV; the afterglow is also the more Stålenhag hour. *If VI:* §4.7's VI brief opens at the farm and its walk in is by the ice road; §3.9's row 12 moves to VI; caption 25 moves with it; the slice builds VI's night row.

### D7. Where chapter VI ends and chapter VII begins (the darkroom morning)

- **VI ends with the walk home across the ice at night and sleep at the farm; VII opens in the darkroom at dawn, developing the facility's negatives, then the walk out along the line** — the Mätrummet print (one more than you counted) is developed by every player on the core route; the night walk home is a new set piece (the Collector's lamp behind you, hulls walking to the ice); VII grows by ten minutes, of which eight are a bunk ride.
- **VI ends in a docked hull's bunk in HALL 3; VII opens there** — the panel's version; the facility's prints are developed only by a player who walks 955 m home and back, which is most of the game's reveals lost for most players.

**Recommendation: the walk home and the darkroom morning.** The hull bunk stays as an optional bed; a player who sleeps there wakes at dawn as the hull walks out, and their facility negatives show as thumbnails in the final album, which is what "They kept some of them" means. *If the hull bunk:* §4.7's briefs revert; §5.3's rule about undeveloped negatives does more work; §3.9's row 25 goes.

### D8. Where a carry wakes you, per chapter

- **Before VI: on the kerb by the bicycle racks outside HALL 3's closed west door, under the foil blanket, in the current chapter's light; from VI: on the bench inside HALL 3; a Collector gather: HALL 1's clerk's chair with the sled in the hopper** — the fiction is the diary's (bearers bring people to the warm halls); HALL 3's interior is kept for chapter VI; the walk home is the cost; a chapter-III player sees the facility yard by daylight with the Collector asleep in it, which seeds it without spending it.
- **The nearest warm room's bed (Johan's room in III, the farm in II and IV, Båten's bunk in V)** — the shortest walk back and a gentler failure; loses the halls as the destination the diary names.
- **HALL 3's bench in every chapter** — the panel's text; spends the game's signature room on the most likely first failure, in chapter III.

**Recommendation: the kerb before VI.** *If the warm-room version:* §4.2's per-site "carry wakes you here" entries change and the bicycles are first seen in VI; §11's slice builds a kitchen bench rather than the kerb. Under any option the morning shot after a carry is composed to show what the night cost, and the one morning line fires there.

### D9. What the night gathers: bounded to the Collector's country

- **Gathered inside the Collector's country, monuments outside** — a bearer switched off or cut in chapters II to V kneels where it stopped for the rest of the game, its number a caption; inside the facility's country it is in the hopper next morning with its number turned toward you; the hopper list is the finite list in §4.6.
- **Gathered everywhere overnight** — the world empties by the quiet route too, everywhere; contradicts the counter against emptiness that switched-off bearers stay where they stood and their prints show the red light still on.

**Recommendation: bounded to the country.** *If everywhere:* §4.6's hopper column loses its "in its country" clause and §8.6's counter 6 is struck.

### D10. The dock umbilicals in HALL 3

- **Cuttable, one hull only (the one nearest the bench, whose umbilical hangs low): twelve seconds of arc unmoors the hull; its breath continues, its window lights, the blue wave skips that dock, and it walks out that night as an extra row in the nights table; no reward; a card appears in the index** — the darkest act in the game is sending someone away, which is this game's kind of dark.
- **Untouchable: umbilicals scorch and never sever** — simplest; the loud route has nothing to do in HALL 3.
- **Cuttable and the breath stops** — the panel's version; the critics called it the point where sad becomes cruel, and it is one step past Dead Space, whose dead were already dead.

**Recommendation: unmooring.** *If untouchable:* §3.7's umbilical seam becomes plate; §4.6 loses its extra row.

### D11. Warmth as chapter VI's inversion, and the sled as the Collector's prey

- **Adopt both** — for 60 s after any stove or heater, bearers notice a warm player through walls and fog within 12 m, and calm cannot rise above 0.5 while warm, so the safest rooms have the walk out that the player fears; the sled's runners over steel (cable trays, grating) are a call at 20 m, so dragging it across the facility yard summons the Collector to the sled, not to you, and the hook takes it.
- **Neither** — stillness is universal from hour three, which the fear critic identified as where the fear collapses; the Collector cannot catch anyone who takes three steps.
- **Warmth only** — the sled stays company and nothing gathers it but the nights table.

**Recommendation: both.** Warmth is the design's own theme (they collect the warm) and the senses table already defines it; the sled as prey gives the stalker something that moves without breaking "nothing hunts". *If neither:* §3.1's warmth sense loses its two clauses; §3.8's call list loses the sled; the nights-table row "the sled, if left north of the fence" is the only way the sled is ever taken.

### D12. The walker bunk ride, sit-only

- **Sit-only: "climb" is a context verb that plays a 3 s ascent and seats you on the bunk bone; the interior is a rigid chunk on the hull bone with no colliders; the stove, the window and the photograph are verbs from the seat; the sled comes along on its rope under the hull** — costs no moving-room collider or camera-sweep system, which does not exist and is not budgeted.
- **Free walking inside a moving hull** — the panel's image; a moving reference frame under the whole collider, portal, camera and audio stack, budgeted nowhere; the M8 test would pass trivially and prove nothing.

**Recommendation: sit-only.** The image survives whole from the seat. *If free walking:* §9 gains a moving-room system at about 800 lines and §9.3's M8 grows by two weeks; it should then be cut-order item 8 as written.

### D13. Documents: three Google Fonts faces or a stroke synthesiser

- **Load one handwriting face and one typewriter face from Google Fonts beside Cormorant Garamond; render the diary, the logbook, the registry cards and the timetable to canvas with per-glyph jitter and pencil grain from the brushes; three hands from one face by slant, size and weight, the third hand's block capitals painted stroke by stroke** — consistent with the first game, which already loads Cormorant; the artifact host allows Google Fonts; a week saved.
- **Paint every letter stroke by stroke, no fonts** — strictly asset-free; a week and 400 lines that may still read wrong; three distinct hands is a research problem.

**Recommendation: fonts, with a system-serif fallback and the lint rendering every document at the 1.35× size to fail on overflow.** Stencils on steel stay painted. *If no fonts:* §9's `text.js` gains a glyph painter and §11 schedules it after the gate.

### D14. Defaults and small forks, taken together

These are minor and are listed so they are visible; each has a recommendation already applied in the text.

- **Calendar 16–21 December 1997 and "ten to three"; the company renamed Mälardalens Kraft AB (MDK)** — recommended (the solstice dawn is literal; *Mellansvensk Kraftgrupp AB* is real). The register check is Julian's; the sandbox could not do it.
- **The sled is a seat anywhere outdoors: saves, sanctuary, one line said once** — recommended, so "you can always sit down" is true on the ice. Alternatives: benches only (the crossings become the one place the promise is false); a seat that does not save (a second kind of sitting to learn).
- **Quiet mode, larger text and reduced motion default on for phones (shortest side under 600 px), stated on the title screen** — recommended, continuing the first game's rule. Alternative: off everywhere and offered in settings.
- **Captions off by default in the first build, revisited after the unseen vocabulary is playtested for spoilers** — recommended. Alternative: on by default, which helps the muted-laptop player most.
- **Safelight amber** — recommended; red spends the colour law's strongest rule in the game's home.
- **The distance smear judged on the crossing still with `?smear=0` beside it; the haze pull is the fallback** — recommended, not really a fork: the still decides.
- **Roos on channel one as a formant murmur under the carrier, with a no-words listening test and a carrier-only fallback** — recommended.
- **The diegetic pad as a one-week experiment in the first culvert with a six-listener go/no-go, never a dependency** — recommended.
- **Bay scale as designed (six- to ten-minute legs)** — recommended; compress uniformly by up to 30 percent only if the eight-week slice shows the crossing outstaying its welcome.
- **Print storage: thumbnail and negative only, the developed print re-derived deterministically** — recommended; storing the developed JPEG too doubles storage and freezes the grade.
- **The 1974 archive photographs as 2D paintings by the first game's brushes, hung in HALL 1; only the four remembered frames are 3D re-renders** — recommended; the full state-parametric scene system moves to cut-order item 3.

## 2. The direction

### 2.1 Premise

Eight winters after the sphere. The narrator of *Stålhagen*, unnamed, ungendered, twenty-one, works for Mälardalens Kraft as a junior line inspector because it was the only job that would send them back. The islands were cut from the grid in the summer of 1991, the summer the machines were pushed into the bay and the towers went cold. This winter the islands are drawing current; some nights the meter at the shore substation runs backwards; two meter readers went across in November and came back saying the lamps were on. A supervisor, Roos, drives the narrator out along the shore road on 16 December 1997 with a sled in the back of the van and stays with the van. "Roos said to be back before dark. I said I would."

The bay froze because the towers stopped warming the water. Why the towers warmed the water is never explained; the game leaves that silence where the first game left it. What the sequel does answer, it answers with objects the player can photograph and never with narration: a registry card in HALL 1 (SV, *skyddsvagn*, shelter wagon; KV, *kraftvagn*, power wagon: civil-defence machines built in 1974 to walk the islands' people off across the ice if the war came, stood down by a form in 1979, and "Finland" the story told to children); a hull standing up out of the bay with ice sliding off it; a meter running the wrong way; a diary at a kitchen table that names the thing at the facility only as *det som svarar*, that which answers.

Black cables lie across the snow where nobody laid them, blue light moving in them slow, like breathing. They run from the facility over the ice into the village, through the birches, down into the hole where SV-14's slab used to be. Where a cable reaches a machine, the machine wakes. The small four-legged ones first: bearers, 04's larger siblings, with a cradle on the back and the same two notes, stopping to look back at you the way 04 did; if you stand still long enough they come to see why, and then they pick you up. Then the walkers.

The islanders who stayed (the old, the stubborn, Johan, his mother) are never seen. They are aboard. In HALL 3 the hulls stand docked in rows and from every hull's vent a plume of breath rises, the blue light breathing through the cables in one slow wave the length of the hall, and on the bench by the door there is a folded foil blanket, a thermos, and room for a third. Kerstin Sandell's diary, read at her kitchen table in Näset, says what they asked for: the towers to keep going, the bus to come back, the winter not to be so long, someone to come, to be warm. The thing that answered the radio and the bell in 1989 answered them. It is hospitable, and hospitality with one answer is worse than malice.

### 2.2 Protagonist

The same narrator, grown, never seen from the front: the camera lives behind the shoulder, the orbit is limited to the rear 200 degrees, the pause and album screens show the back of a head in a yellow knit hat, and the viewfinder is first person. A new orange parka in the same colour ("the only one they had in my size"), Mom's yellow hat, blue-grey work trousers, an MDK armband, a head torch, a sled on a rope. The voice is unchanged: first person, past tense, short declarative pairs, the second undercutting the first, no fear words, adults quoted and never shown. Stated reason for going: the job. Real reason: eight years of looking at photographs for a small red light.

Kit, complete on day one and never upgraded: a voltage tester on the belt that ticks near current (the motion tracker, passive and diegetic), a line-worker's arc cutter, a hand winch, six flares, the head torch, and a compact 35 mm camera with a flash. Torch, cutter and flash draw on one battery pool, so light is ammunition and every use of it is a call. Body state is the HUD: snow on the shoulders, breath, wet legs after the marsh, frost creeping in from the hem when cold, a limp after a grapple, ice on the lens after a fall. No bars, no numbers, no map but the paper one on the kitchen wall, which you can photograph and then look at anywhere.

### 2.3 World

One winter, one bay, eight places joined by ice. A hub-and-spokes map: the frozen bay is the corridor and each site is a dense room, visited in an authored order and freely revisited, with Sjögården the hub because Dad's darkroom is in its cellar. Fastlandet (the van, the substation) → Isen (the crossing on the 1974 winter road) → Sjögården (the sold house with its window lit again, the kitchen table, the darkroom, the hole where the slab was) → Näset (the bus shelter, ten red houses with foil taped inside the windows, the shop, the school, Johan's house and the diary) → Björkhagen (KV-3 asleep under cables; the 1989 sensor head; the twelve heads in the birches) → Kärret and Båten (the safe house; Västerskär reachable across the ice for the first time; the bell) → Anläggningen (the hole in the fence, the yard of bicycles, HALL 1's registry, HALL 2's workshop, the culverts, Mätrummet, HALL 3, the sphere yard) → Isvägen (the line of walkers walking out to the edge where Klotet hangs over black water) → Islossningen, April. Kyltornen, the towers, are a horizon and a stretch chapter; if not built they stay unexplained, which the game prefers. Revisits use the first game's epilogue trick at least four times: the birches with a KV-3-shaped clearing, the village after the last islander is fetched, the snow field with walkers standing in the bay behind the house, the bay empty in April.

A colour law, enforced in the renderer: blue is the facility's charge (cables, seams, pipes, the ice lit from below); red is what watches and answers (LEDs, the mast, reflectors that light only in your beam); sodium and lamp-yellow are human warmth, and warm light is where you are safe; a flare's warm white is a human's light. Nothing bleeds. The only thing that ever comes out of anything is light.

### 2.4 Horror

Everything answers. Every act of power or noise is a call: the torch, a flash, a cut, a flare, a switch, a bell, the radio, running on steel, the sled's runners over a cable tray. Ten seconds after you plug into a socket a blue pulse starts down the nearest cable toward you, and when it arrives the corridor is live for a minute; you decide how long to stay. Nothing hunts. Danger is a posture, not a place: wires come to light and sound and never leave their anchor length; bearers come to stillness and to warmth; walkers walk their routes to the ice and do not turn toward you; the heads come to the torch; the Collector answers sound, 3.6 seconds late, closer each time, and cannot enter a hall.

The cast, all boxes, cylinders, tubes, spheres and chains on one skeleton: scouts (04's size, harmless, leading you to a bench or to a bearer); bearers (1.4 m at the shoulder, an instrument-case body with one orange stripe, a cradle of tubes, grapple arms folded under the chin, a red-LED head, a sodium work lamp that comes on the moment it decides to come); wires (verlet chains lying in snow and hanging from pipes, a live end that lifts and looks); walkers (seventeen to fifty metres, one footfall every six seconds, hulls that are warm shelters with a bunk and a stove); small spheres (0.8 m, drifting the wires' routes; within eight metres snow falls upward and the sled floats); the heads (twelve discarded sensor heads on wire legs in the birches, lighting one at a time and swarming the torch); and Hämtaren, the Collector, SV-14's own legs carrying a crane, a sodium lamp and a hopper, gathering whatever was left loud in its country. By day it stands in the scrapyard with snow on its hopper and you can put your hand on its foot. It is Dad's machine.

Why they are sad: they are 04's family and they beep for you; they were built to save people and they are succeeding, thirty years late, with whoever could not leave. Switching off a bearer means holding still while it walks to you, reaching out, and turning off a red light you have been looking for in photographs for eight years.

### 2.5 Core loop

Minute to minute: walk, listen (the tester ticks, the ice sings in HRTF, a beep in fog, feet on snow behind you), and make one decision constantly: torch on or off, moving or still. A hidden calm value rises while you stand still without light or sound and falls with every step, cut, flare and switch. High calm is when a bearer trusts you enough to come within reach, and its distance from you is the only meter. The standoff: it is within a metre, facing you, its lamp on; the prompt says *reach out*; a two-second hold; its head turns to your hand; at one second its arms twitch open; the LED goes the dark red of the sensor head in the birches; the first game's four-note touch chord in a minor mode. Let go and nothing happens except that nothing resets. Jerk the stick, hurry, light the torch, raise the cutter, and it takes you instead: six slow seconds, the camera on the sky, snow on the lens, the two notes. Combat as a contest of patience: the strongest move is to wait, and waiting while a thing walks toward you is the scariest thing the game does.

The loud route: every machine's bone tree is a current graph, charge flowing as blue light along seams from its contact (a foot on a live cable, its belly reservoir) to its limbs. The cutter severs where the blue is: cut a seam and everything downstream goes grey and the limb drops (that bone's triangles removed, the part a ragdoll chunk in the snow for good); a bearer on three legs limps, on two drags itself on its grapples; cut the belly seam under its chin and the whole thing goes dark from the belly outward, stops, turns its head to you, waits three seconds, and the red light goes off. The environment is half the arsenal: a JORD breaker grounds a floor for thirty seconds and kills everything standing on it; crates and drifts are moats to wires; a flare dropped is a light nobody is holding; the winch pins a bearer's leg so that for twelve seconds its arms are busy with the hook and its belly is still. Violence spends anonymity: every cut is a call, wakes the day's wires within forty metres, and lowers the calm ceiling until you sleep, so bearers stop trusting stillness and the quiet route closes behind the loud one. Nothing respawns. Nothing you do is required to kill anything; a bot proves every chapter by the quiet route and by the loud one.

Resources, kept small: one battery pool; flares, finite; seventies first-aid boxes; warmth as frost from the hem, restored near any heat. Inventory is the sled: everything lives on it, it follows on a rope with real friction, floats in a sphere's field, and stays outside interiors, where you carry six slots into the dark. Switchboards are the first game's lighting circuit grown into a grammar: sending power to a lift or a door makes the corridor between board and door live and its wires wake ten seconds later, so you plan a route like a line worker and walk it in the dark; a fuse in a room's box lights the room for good, and lit rooms are the only permanent progression and the only permanent sanctuary. The islands get a little brighter.

Saving and failure. Sit down: benches, kitchen chairs, a tractor seat, the bus shelter, the crow's nest, a walker's bunk, the sled anywhere outdoors. Sitting pulls the camera back to a composed wide shot, says the place's line if it has one, saves silently, and is absolute: nothing approaches a sitting player ("You can always sit down." on the pause screen). Sleep, at beds, spends a night. Being carried is not death and is not free: the bearer lifts you over six slow seconds (cut the arm at the shoulder seam, or let it), and you wake under a foil blanket with your things in a crate, the sled parked, and a line in Johan's logbook in a hand that is not his and not yours: FOUND ONE ON THE ICE. BROUGHT IN. The night has passed. Being carried spends a night the way sleeping does, and the world moves at night by a finite table: one more house in Näset goes dark, one more hull walks out onto the bay, one more card in the registry. That is the game's only pressure and it is applied by your own failure, never by a clock. Real death exists for four causes only: a fall over six metres, thin ice, a walker's foot, a live arc at a wire's anchor. A death is a cold fade of 1.6 seconds to the last seat, no counter, no line, no lost photograph.

### 2.6 Contemplation

Protected by rules, not mood, and each rule has a test (§10). No timers and no clock: each chapter's light is fixed and days advance only when you sleep or are carried. Nothing spawns, nothing is placed behind you, everything that will threaten you is announced by sound before it moves, and the tester ticks two seconds before anything takes its first step. No stingers, no chase, no QTE, no arena, no forced capture, no forced kill. Nothing is louder than a walker's footfall. ## 3. Creatures, combat and the standoff

This section decides the bestiary, the two routes through it, and the one thing that comes for you. It owns every creature number in the game; §8 and §11 quote them. Four shared systems come first so the creatures can be short: a skeleton, a limb scheduler, a sense model, and a current graph. The player walks at 1.4 m/s on snow (1.3 with the sled), 1.6 on ice, 1.2 on boards; runs at 2.4 on snow and 2.8 on ice, capped at 2.2 with the sled on ice. Nothing here closes distance on a moving player faster than a quarter of walking speed except the walkers and the Collector, and neither of those ever turns toward you.

### 3.1 Shared systems

**Skeleton.** Every creature is a bone tree (parent index, local TRS, world matrix, inverse bind) of at most 64 bones; every rig in the game is under 48. Bones live in one shared RGBA32F texture, 256 × 128 texels: four texels per bone, one row per skinned entity, rows taken from a free list, 512 KB, double-buffered across frames so an upload never touches a texture the GPU is reading. The vertex shader reads its row from a per-draw uniform and the texture's size from `textureSize()`, so no bone count is baked into any shader. Skinned vertices carry two extra u8vec4 attributes (indices, weights) beyond the 36-byte packed layout; rigid parts use one bone at weight 1, and only hoses, the parka hood and the walkers' hip cables use four weights. The Builder's per-vertex bone tag becomes the triangle's owner bone (owner = first vertex), and the index buffer is sorted by owner at build so every bone has a start and a count. That range is the whole basis of severing. Per-bone glow rides in the unused fourth-row components of each affine matrix, so there is no second array.

**Limb scheduler.** One scheduler drives every leg and arm in the game. A limb is {base offset in body frame, rest offset, l1, l2, pole, state, world target}. A planted limb keeps a fixed world target while the body moves (the planted-foot-never-slides rule from `peds.js`, generalised); a swinging limb moves its target along a raised cosine over `swingT`, lifting `swingH`. A limb asks to step when its foot is more than stride/2 behind its rest position in the body frame. Permission: at a walk only one limb swings at a time, in a fixed order; at a trot (scouts only) diagonal pairs may swing together. Body height is the mean planted-foot height plus the rest height; body pitch and roll come from a plane fit of the planted feet, filtered with a 0.3 s time constant, falling back to the previous plane when fewer than three feet are planted, so a machine tilts on a drift instead of floating over it and does not jerk at every handover. Each limb is solved by `solveLimb` with its own pole vector, and the same code moves a bearer's arm to drag it across the snow when it has no legs. Secondary motion (antennae, hoses, the lamp yoke, the hood) is spring-damper, `x'' = -k(x - rest) - c x'`, with k and c per part, integrated at the fixed step.

**Senses.** Four stimuli, all read from the per-frame `W.noise` list and three cheap tests. Only the player and the player's tools are stimuli; machines never rouse each other. *Light:* a creature is lit if the torch cone (the rendered cone, outer 26°, 22 m) or the flash (12 m of light, 25 m of startle, one frame) reaches it with `sight3` clear. Room lamps on a fused circuit are not a light stimulus. *Sound:* every noise on the list has a radius = surface base × √mass, doubled when running; bases: snow 8, ice 14, steel 20, boards 10; the player's mass is 1; a shutter is 4, a winch click 12, a switch or breaker 25, a flare lit 30, the radio playing 30 continuous, a cut 40, a door pulled shut 20, the sled's runners over steel 20. Every noise carries y and a room id, and a listener hears it only if the portal-path distance (§7.5's search, computed once per noise per room) is within the radius, halved by each closed door. *Warmth:* the player is warm within 6 m always (breath), and within 12 m, through walls and fog, for 60 s after standing at any stove or heater; while warm, calm cannot rise above 0.5. *Stillness:* a player at under 0.1 m/s in line of sight under 20 m is interesting after 2 s and worth a visit after 5 s (the Robot's five seconds, unchanged). A sitting player triggers no sense.

**Current graph.** Every machine's bone tree is also its wiring. Each bone carries parent, `seamLen` (metres of seam from the parent's joint), `phaseDist` (seam distance from the reservoir), `severed`, `charge`, `glow` and `cap` (seconds of held charge). Sources are the reservoir bone while the reservoir is above zero, and any foot bone standing on a live cable or floor. Per frame: clear charge, flood-fill from the sources over unsevered parent-child edges, then glow chases charge with a time constant of 0.25 s rising and 0.8 s falling, and the per-bone emissive is `glow × (0.35 + 0.65 × pulse(phaseDist - 1.6 t))`, a 1.2 m wide pulse every 4 m travelling from the belly outward at 1.6 m/s. That is the blue light moving slow in the seams, and it costs forty integers a frame. The hum and the tester read the same numbers (§7.3). The graph publishes a per-frame list of charged elements with positions and pulse phases and emits `onSever` with the affected graph.

**The reservoir.** A bearer holds twenty minutes of walking off any source and recharges at 5 percent per second on any live cable or floor. At 15 percent it leaves whatever it is doing (Route, Wait, even a standoff) and walks at cruise to the nearest live cable to stand on it, with a visible tell: the belly seam dim, the two notes a semitone flat. Turning a corridor off is therefore a real tactic with a delay; the bot asserts that no bearer stands more than twenty-five minutes off a source.

### 3.2 The bestiary

**04 and the scouts.** Body 0.50 × 0.28 × 0.28 m, a beige instrument case with one orange stripe and a two-digit stencil in the 1989 typeface; shoulder height 0.42 m. Sixteen bones: root, body, head, two antenna segments, and four legs of coxa, femur (0.22) and tibia (0.20). The head is a 0.12 m box with a black window and a 12 mm red LED; the window is flagged retroreflective, so it lights only in your beam. Blink 3 Hz idle, 9 Hz moving, exactly the Robot. Gait: a trot at 0.8 m/s, and the hop: a ballistic arc of 0.6 to 1.2 m with a 0.25 m apex, legs tucked, a 0.1 s crouch on landing. Behaviour is the Robot ported to metres: a waypoint list per placement; it waits when you are more than 6 m behind; when you come within 4 m it looks at you for 0.7 s and hops ahead; if you run at it it goes at once with the startled beep (1.4×); it beeps every 4 to 7 s when you are more than 9 m behind; if you stand still 5 s and are 4 to 15 m away over walkable ground it comes back to within 1.5 m to see why, with a 20 s cooldown, and returns to its post when you move 1.5 m or exceed 1.2 m/s. Antenna spring k 60, c 3. Sounds: the two square notes 1320/1760 Hz at 90/120 ms; four light ticks per step on snow. Senses: stillness and light; it ignores sound. It does nothing to the player. What it does to the game: every scout leads somewhere, a bench or a bearer, decided per placement and never random; and at 30 m in fog it has the same silhouette as a bearer, so the first thing you learn is to count blinks. 04 is a scout with the stencil 04, visible only in the viewfinder until the edge of the ice, beeping in HRTF from where it waits, and it comes back to see why whether or not the camera is raised.

**Bearers.** Shoulder 1.4 m; case 1.6 × 0.8 × 0.7 m, rimed, one orange stripe, a two-digit number you cannot read until it is close enough to read yours; the registry files them as B. Forty bones: root; body; a belly pack (the reservoir housing, whose seam under the case is the brightest seam on the machine); a cradle of eight tubes on the back, hinged, tilting back 20° to load; neck and head (0.32 × 0.22 × 0.20 m, black window, 40 mm LED, retroreflective); lamp yoke and lamp (a sodium work lamp on a stalk at the front left corner, 0.2 m dish, yoke spring k 25, c 4); two antenna segments (k 60, c 3, red tip); two arms of shoulder, upper (0.55), fore (0.50) and grapple (a two-finger clamp, 0.25), folded under the chin at rest like a mantis praying, reach 1.3 m; four legs of coxa, femur (0.62) and tibia (0.58) with a 0.12 m rubber pad; two hydraulic hoses from the case to the rear hips, four bones each (k 18, c 2.5). Gait: a walk only, order LH, LF, RH, RF, `swingT` 0.42 s, `swingH` 0.12 m, cruise 0.55 m/s, approach 0.35 m/s. The pole vectors point outward and forward, so the knees splay slightly: mechanical, not canine. Head look-at lags with `1 - 0.02^dt`, the Robot's constant, which at this size reads as considering. Every 14 to 22 s on a route it stops, turns its head 120° back toward you, holds 0.7 s, sounds the two notes an octave below a scout's (660/880 Hz, 180/240 ms) and walks on: the hop-wait-look-back without the hop. Senses: all four. Sounds: the notes; the hum by distance; a 100 Hz ballast buzz when the lamp is on; a compressed-snow crunch and a servo whine per foot; a hydraulic sigh when the body lowers. Blink 1 Hz. Each carries one thing in its cradle, visible when it kneels: a folded blanket, a dog's collar, a child's boot, a Sunday paper from 1996, the shop's last order book, a pair of reading glasses, a thermos lid, a hymn book; you may take one thing from a kneeling cradle. The look-dev criteria for sad-not-silly, judged on an eight-second clip (walk 6 m in fog, stop, look back, beep, walk on) by people who did not build it: legs longer than the body is tall; the head carried below the back line and raised only to look; arms folded; nothing on it pointed at you except the lamp; no motion shorter than 0.4 s except the flinch; no visible jerk at a leg handover. First fix if silly: slow everything 25 percent. Second: lower the head.

**Wires.** Verlet chains of 24 to 48 links at 0.25 m (6 to 12 m), anchored at a flange, drum or socket, with a live end: a 0.12 m ferrule with a blue seam ring. No skeleton; the tube mesh is rebuilt from the chain into a dynamic buffer each frame, so it can be cut anywhere. States: *lying* (in the dark; the tester ticks within 6 m at a rate of 1/d); *rousing* (light within 8 m or sound within its radius: the last four links lift the head 0.6 m over 2.0 s and turn to the stimulus; a dry dragging sound, HRTF-placed, is the tell, 2.0 s before it can move); *reaching* (the head moves along the surface at 1.2 m/s toward the stimulus, links dragging, never beyond anchor length L, and past 0.6 L its speed is scaled by `1 - ((d - 0.6L)/(0.4L))²` so it is visibly pulled up short); *strike* (stimulus within 0.8 m: a 2.6 kHz crackle 0.45 s before, as the 1989 arcs, then a 0.3 s lunge and the last six links wrap a leg). Touch: a 0.6 s stun, the torch dead for 8 s, 15 percent of battery gone, frost from the hem. A wrap is a hold, not a countdown: the wire drags the wrapped leg toward its anchor at 0.3 m/s, the hum climbing to 110 Hz and the tester bursting as you go, and the only death is contact with the socket or flange at the anchor, 6 to 12 m away, so twenty to forty seconds. Break a wrap by cutting any link (0.3 s of arc: downstream goes limp and grey at once, upstream retracts), by dropping a flare (it goes to the brighter light within 8 m), or by being dragged over wood or snow deeper than 5 cm, which the head cannot cross and which breaks the wrap: steel is theirs and wood is ours. Under quiet mode the drag stops at 0.6 L and the wire holds you until you cut. The trunk cables lying across the snow are roads, not wires; they never move. Wires live on steel, concrete, and ice lit blue from below.

**Walkers.** Two classes: SV, 9 m legs under a 12 × 7 × 5 m hull with the hip beam at 12 m and the hull top at 17 m; KV, 50 m. Sixteen bones: root, hull, hip, thigh, shin and foot per leg, two pistons per leg, the dead eye, the vent; the hip cables are verlet chains of thirty 1 m links dragging on the ice. The scheduler at a 6 s period: swing 2.4 s, foot lifted 3 m, stride 14 m at cruise, double support 3.6 s, the hull swaying 0.8 m over the planted leg. Cruise 2.3 m/s, faster than you walk; on the line in chapter VII the stride shortens to 7 m and the pace to 1.2 m/s, a procession, and the line stands still for a minute every 300 m. They walk their routes to the ice and never turn toward you. The tells, in order: the ice under the landing point lights blue from below 2 s before the foot arrives (the under-ice light list); the piston creak 1.5 s before; the footfall, a 30 Hz thump and an ice crack, the loudest sound in the game. Being under a 4 × 3 m pad when it lands is a real death; walkers are routed so no footfall ever lands within 4 m of a seat, and under quiet mode a walker whose next landing point is within 4 m of the player holds its step with a hydraulic sigh until the player clears. The hulls are warm: a hip cable is climbable by a context verb (a 3 s scripted ascent onto the bunk bone; the interior is one rigid chunk on the hull bone, no colliders); inside is a bunk, a stove and a window, all verbs from the seat; the bunk is a sit, absolute, and the walker keeps walking while you watch the ice pass, the sled sliding along below on its rope. In HALL 3 the docked hulls hang from ceiling umbilicals, which are contacts on the graph.

**Small spheres.** 0.8 m, one bone, seams and six dim equator lights in the Klotet colour, a warm rim from the sky. They drift along the trunk cables at 0.5 m/s, 1.6 m up. The field is one function in `world.js`, `g(r)`: gravity multiplier 1 beyond 8 m, easing to 0.35 at 3 m, and −0.3 inside 2 m, read by particles, the sled, the player and audio's subtraction rule. Snow falls upward, the sled floats to its rope length, you fall slowly, and within 2 m your feet leave the ice for a metre and are set down. No sound but the snow's whisper stopping and a 220 Hz tone within 3 m. They do nothing to you, and their field is the one place a bearer cannot stand: its planted feet lose the plane fit, the scheduler refuses every step, and it waits at the edge of the field. The sphere is the game's warm-light sanctuary that moves.

**The heads.** Twelve discarded sensor heads in the birches at Björkhagen: a bearer's head box, mossy, on three wire legs of two 0.5 m links; seven bones each. They are the 1989 lighting circuit with legs. Dormant until the torch or the flash hits one; then the signal sound (a 600 Hz burst and a 110 Hz sine for 1.7 s), it lights (LED off-red to red), and 1.1 s later the nearest unlit head lights, and so on down the grove, one at a time, the way the old circuit lit the lamps. A lit head walks toward the light at 0.4 m/s on a tripod gait (one leg at a time, `swingT` 0.3 s): a spider from far, a head walking from near. They swarm the torch: they gather in the cone and stand in it, windows turned to the light, and do nothing to you. Torch off: they wait 5 s and walk back in reverse order, going dark one by one. What they cost: twelve boxes in your beam, and their light is a light, so the bearer asleep under KV-3 comes to it. Any leg seam is cuttable (0.6 s); a head with one leg drags in a circle; cut the neck stub and it is a head in the snow again, where it was in 1989. In audio the twelve are one swarm source.

**Hämtaren, the Collector.** SV-14's own legs, 9 m, the stencil SV-14 RIKSVÄGSVERKET still legible on the hip beam, one shin welded in a different colour (the leg that stood up out of the snow at an angle in 1989), 12 m to the beam, carrying a crane arm of two booms (8 and 6 m), a sodium work lamp at the elbow, a red aviation light at the tip, and a 4 × 3 × 2 m hopper hung between the legs. Twenty bones plus a thirty-link verlet hook cable with a lamp on the hook. It walks at one footfall per 3 s, 6 m strides, 2 m/s. It has no eyes; it hears, and the lamp is what it looks with. Its lamp is sodium because it is a rescue machine and its light is hospitable; the colour law holds: being gathered is safe. It stands at its post day and night, lamp off, and moves only in reply. By day it stands in the scrapyard with snow on its hopper and you can put your hand on its foot; at night in chapter VI its lamp comes on at your first call. Full logic in §3.8.

### 3.3 Calm

A hidden value c in [0, 1]. While still (under 0.1 m/s), torch off, and no player noise in the last second, c rises at 0.06 per second, seventeen seconds from nothing to full. Walking: −1/3 per second. Running: −1 per second. Torch on: −1/4 per second, additive with movement. A flash: −0.4. A flare lit: −0.5. A switch thrown: −0.3. A cut: c = 0. Warm (§3.1): c is clamped at 0.5. Sitting freezes c and does not matter, because sitting is absolute. c is clamped to a ceiling A, the day's anonymity (§3.7). There is no meter; the meter is the bearer's distance.

**What calm does.** A bearer that has noticed you walks toward you at 0.35 m/s until its distance is at or under `stopDist = 0.8 + 5.2 × (1 − min(c, A))`. At c = 1 it comes to 0.8 m, inside reach. At c = 0.5 it stops at 3.4 m and waits with its lamp on and its head on you. Hold still and c rises and it walks the rest of the way: the approach is your own calm made visible. Move and it stops; walk away and it follows at 0.35 m/s, about a quarter of your speed, so you can always walk away from a bearer, and it loses you at 30 m or after 12 s out of sight and returns to its post. Nothing hunts. But while you walk c cannot rise, and when you stop it closes, so the decision is never postponed, only carried. As readable distances: A 1.0 → 0.8 m; 0.85 → 1.6 m; 0.7 → 2.4 m; 0.55 → 3.1 m; 0.25 → 4.7 m.

### 3.4 Bearer states

*Dormant:* under cables, everything off; woken by a scripted cable pulse at placements that call for it, and the tester has ticked for 2.0 s before it moves. *Route:* 0.55 m/s, the look-back every 14 to 22 s. *Noticed:* it notices a still player at 25 m in daylight or in your beam and at 12 m in dusk or night; head to you at 0.25 s per 90°, blink 1 Hz to 2 Hz, lamp off, the tester ticking faster; it holds 2.0 s, the Robot's 0.7 s look scaled to its size. *Approach:* the lamp comes on at the instant it decides, at whatever distance, and that is the earliest tell; 0.35 m/s toward you to `stopDist`; every 6 s it pauses 0.7 s, lifts its head, sounds the two notes and walks on. *Wait:* at `stopDist`, lamp on, head on you, one step in place every 6 s, the antenna whipping once as it stops; it will wait forever. *Standoff:* distance at or under 1.0 m and its heading within 30° of you; the prompt fades in 0.4 s later. *Taking* (§3.6). *Off:* the LED goes to the dark red of the head in the birches (#5b2a22), the lamp fades over 2 s, all four femurs fold 25° and the body drops 0.3 m (the kneel), the antenna spring goes to k 5 and droops, the hoses go slack, the cradle tilts and what it carries can be read. It stays there for good outside the Collector's country, snow settles on it the way it settles on your shoulders, and you can photograph the number; the print carries the number as its caption and shows the red light still on.

**Sitting.** A sitting player triggers no sense. A bearer that was already engaged holds where it is if farther than 3 m; if closer it steps back to 3 m over its own time and waits, lamp on. When you stand, the standoff resumes from there.

**Inside reach.** Inside 1.3 m a bearer either has your hand or takes you. At any time inside reach, hurry, torch on, the flash, the cutter raised or a flare lit is a flinch. Walking (not hurrying) away is not: it follows. Progress on any of its seams snaps its head to the arc (the 0.12 s motion), whips the antenna and fails that leg's next step; a standing bearer allows one 0.9 s sever before it grips, and the second sever is the shoulder seam during the lift. Clipping the winch to a leg inside reach is permitted without a flinch (0.5 s, the hook in the other hand); §3.7 says what that buys.

### 3.5 The two seconds

Entry: the bearer in Wait at 1.0 m, facing, lamp on. The camera eases 0.3 m closer over the right shoulder so the hand is in frame at the lower right. The prompt *reach out* fades in over 0.4 s at the foot of the frame in the narration face, not a button glyph. Keyboard: hold E, or Space. Gamepad: hold the south button. Touch: hold the single interact/photo button, which now reads REACH and grows. The hold runs 2.0 s and the arm rises with the ending's smoothstep. At 0.0 the bearer's head begins to turn from your face to your hand with a 0.35 s lag; its servo tick lands at 0.35. At 0.6 the blink stops: steady red. At 1.0 its arms unfold a fifth of the way and the grapples open, with the hydraulic hiss and the ratchet: a twitch, and it is reaching too; on a gamepad, one 40 ms pulse. At 1.5 the hand touches the head and the first game's touch chord sounds with its major third lowered: E, B, E, G over the low E, at 0.18 s intervals, the last note landing at 2.04. At 2.0 the relay tink, the ballast stops, the LED goes off-red and the kneel begins; its contact leaves the charged list and the hum sinks toward 55 Hz over 2.5 s. During the hold the tester's rumble and belt LED are suspended so the one pulse at the twitch is felt. Nothing on screen tells you the time. You count the notes. The twitch is at 1.0 s for the first three standoffs and hashed to 0.7 or 1.3 s per placement after, so the hold never becomes a rhythm.

**Letting go.** Releasing the hold at any time is not a flinch: the hand withdraws over 0.4 s and the bearer waits. Nothing resets: if you let go after the twitch its arms stay a fifth unfolded with the grapples open, and the next hold starts from there with the twitch 0.3 s earlier. Looking around, raising the camera without the flash, and the shutter are not flinches either. There is no timer on the standoff; inaction is always safe.

**A limbless bearer** always accepts reach out: with its arms gone nothing can take you, so the prompt appears at 1.0 m without a twitch, the head turns to the hand, the minor chord, the LED off. The quiet verb closes the loud act.

### 3.6 Failing

A flinch is a sustained movement input over 120 ms above a 0.35 deadzone while the hold is active, or, at any time inside reach, hurry, torch on, the flash, the cutter raised or a flare lit. On a flinch the head snaps back to your face in 0.12 s, the only fast motion the bearer has; the arms unfold fully in 0.8 s, the grapples close on the parka in 0.4 s, and the lift begins: six seconds, the camera on the sky, snow on the lens, the two notes at 60 percent. During the lift the shoulder seam of the lifting arm is in frame at 0.6 m; 0.9 s of arc severs it and you drop half a metre with a limp for 120 s or until any heat or a first-aid box. Or let it, and wake under the foil blanket with the logbook line (§4.6 says where, per chapter). In quiet mode Taking is disabled: a flinch makes the bearer step back one metre and wait, and calm resets.

**Why it is not a QTE.** There is no window to hit; the input is to hold one thing and do nothing else, and the only way to fail is to do something else. The failure is not a reflex failure but a nerve failure, and the twitch at 1.0 s exists to cause it: to make the hand on the stick move. No prompt flashes, no fail sound, no retry; the consequence is itself slow and escapable by another slow act. The two seconds are the last two of a decision you made a minute earlier when you stopped walking and watched it come. The camera does not cut. The wrong-mode chord, the head turning to your hand, the steady LED and the opening grapples are all the same information, that it is reaching too, and the game never says whether it means to be touched or to take you.

**After.** A switch-off makes no noise and spends no anonymity. It is not free: the night table counts switched-off bearers exactly as it counts cut and grounded ones, and inside the Collector's country the machine is gone from where you left it the next morning and is in the hopper in the scrapyard with the number turned toward you. Outside its country it kneels where it stopped for the rest of the game. The world gets emptier by the quiet route too.

### 3.7 The loud route

**The cutter.** A line-worker's arc cutter: a handle with two electrodes 0.3 m apart; the arc jumps to the nearest seam within 0.9 m of the tip, which is inside a bearer's reach; that is the design. Raise (right mouse, left trigger, or the touch RAISE toggle) pulls the camera to Dead Space distance over 0.3 s (0.9 m behind the shoulder, FOV 55° to 45°). There is no reticle; the sight is the arc itself, a 30 cm blue line at the tip, and a seam glows white when it is within 0.18 m of the arc and in range. The hand breathes: a 0.4 Hz sway of 0.15 m amplitude, larger with frost, so the arc only makes progress while it is on the seam, and progress decays at 0.5 per second when it is not; a 2.0 s seam takes about 3.3 s of contact from a standing hand. Fire (left mouse, right trigger, the same on-glass button) arcs. Aim assist for gamepad and touch is a soft magnet to the nearest seam within 6°, on by default there and off for the mouse. Sever times: a wire link 0.3, a scout seam 0.5, a head's leg 0.6, a bearer limb or neck seam 0.9, a bearer belly seam 2.0 (the reservoir housing is thicker, and it is under the chin), the low dock umbilical in HALL 3 12.0. Battery: torch 0.15 percent per second (eleven minutes continuous), flash 4 percent a shot, arc 6 percent a second, so a full battery is sixteen seconds of arc, about twelve bearer seams. A live socket refills at 5 percent per second.

**Sockets and the pulse.** Ten seconds after you plug in, a pulse starts down the nearest cable toward the socket at 1.6 m/s. When it arrives the socket's corridor is live for 60 s: wires within 4 m of the cable wake, dormant bearers with a foot on it wake, the tester bursts six; and the pulse repeats every 30 s while you stay plugged in. Sockets are placed: one per hall on the wall farthest from its door (H1's north wall, H2's east end by the lift, H3's east end by S3), SW-C at J3, Båten's galley, the farm kitchen, the shop; none in Mätrummet. Charging is always a walk into the dark and a decision about wires, never a queue at a wall.

**Targets.** The cutter hits a per-bone capsule built from each bone's geometry bounds. A hit within 0.18 m of that bone's joint targets that seam; within 0.18 m of a child's joint targets the child's seam; anywhere else is plate, which scorches (a burn decal, a call, nothing more). Two kinds of target matter. A limb seam drops the limb. The belly seam is the contact: cutting it disconnects the reservoir, and the graph goes dark from the belly outward over 1.2 s in the order of `phaseDist`; the head's capacitor holds 3 s, so the bearer stops, turns its head to you, waits, and then the red light goes off. That is the whole thing dying, and it happens with you underneath it.

**The sever.** At full progress on seam k: mark `severed[k]`; the subtree S of k is dropped from the live index array (a concatenation of the unsevered bones' ranges, rebuilt once per cut); two cap sets authored per joint at build (a stump ring and disc owned by the parent, a chunk cap owned by k, painted as the inside of a case: dark grey, a ring of copper wire ends, one blue dot) are added to their respective index arrays; and a chunk entity is spawned sharing the vertex buffer with an index array of S plus its cap, driven by a verlet body of one point per joint in S plus the tip, links at bone length with grandparent bracing, gravity, ground push-out and 0.55 friction, exactly `stepRagdoll` with fewer points, bones refit with `fitBone`. The chunk sleeps after 0.6 s still; when it sleeps it is baked (bone matrices applied on the CPU) into the site's static debris mesh, its bone row and draw freed, and it stays for good: a foot in the snow is a photograph. A severed head keeps its 3 s capacitor and blinks in the snow, then stops. Sound: an 80 ms crackle, the part landing on the footfall bus, and the two notes from the head, quieter. No scream, no fluid; only light comes out of anything. The stump's cap glows blue 1.5 s, then grey, and the chunk's glow falls to nothing at the 0.8 s constant: downstream darkening is the same lerp that lights the seams, run the other way. The cradle spills: what the bearer carried lies among the parts.

**A limbless bearer keeps moving.** The scheduler handles four, three, two, one and no legs. Three legs: the leg leaves the order, the body corner over the missing leg drops 0.18 m so the case tilts, the strict one-swing rule holds, stride shortens to 0.7× and speed to 0.6×; the limp is the tilt plus the gap in the rhythm, a three-beat gait with a rest where the fourth beat was. Two legs, any pair: it cannot stand; the case settles on the snow (body height 0.15 m), the legs push like oars, and the arms unfold and become limbs with the role *drag*: each arm reaches 0.9 m ahead, plants the grapple, and while planted the root translates toward the grapple as the arm folds, at 0.25 m/s. One or no legs: arms only, 0.15 m/s. No arms and fewer than two legs: it cannot move, and it lies with its head up following you, beeping every 4 to 7 s, and it accepts reach out. It is meant to be judged sad; if it reads cruel, that is the player's to carry, and the touch is how they put it down; if it reads silly, slow it, judged in the same clip as the gait. Inside the country the Collector gathers it that night.

**The winch.** A hand come-along with 12 m of cable and a hook. Clip the hook to a leg, a socket, a crate or a door handle (0.5 s, within 1 m); clip the drum to a post, pylon, hull, tree or ring (within 1 m); crank 0.4 m per 0.6 s, each crank a click with radius 12. Taut against a bearer's leg, the winch pins: the taut leg cannot swing, the scheduler refuses it, the body corner kneels 0.18 m, and the bearer's arms go to the hook to free it, which takes twelve seconds. For those twelve seconds its belly seam is still and exposed under its chin, and its head is on the hook, not on you. No fall. The loud route's whole argument is that paragraph: hold, clip, walk to a post, crank until taut, twelve seconds, two seconds of arc under its chin, the head turns, the light goes out. Four cranks pull a wall socket out and its corridor's wires go dark for good, the quietest disconnection in the game. A crate pulled across a wire's route is a moat. A door can be pulled shut from 12 m so you are not at the door when it is a call.

**Flares.** Six in the kit, two in each of about eight seventies civil-defence boxes, never more than ten carried. They burn warm white for 90 s, a human's light; lighting one is a call at 30 m, once, and after that it is a light with nobody holding it. Wires and heads go to the brightest light within 8 m, so a flare dropped is a route cleared. A flare thrown onto rotten ice melts through and goes out with a hiss: the ice's tell. Machines do not read a flare as warmth, which is what heatless means.

**Breakers as grounding.** Facility breaker boxes have a handle with three positions, 0, I and JORD, and a fuse slot. A fuse in the slot with the handle at I lights the room's lamp circuit for good; it is not on any board, it wakes nothing, and a lit room is sanctuary (§4.4). JORD, present on six boxes by design (H1, SW-A, H2, SW-B, J2, J4; never Mätrummet), grounds that section's floor plates for 30 s and then trips to 0 on a spring: every machine with a foot on the floor has its reservoir shorted through its feet at 20 percent per second, and in five seconds it is Off, the kneel and the dark red, same as a touch. It is the loud route's only kill at a distance, it is a call at 25 m, and it kills everything on the floor, scouts included. No door or lift is ever on a floor section's circuit, so grounding is always tactical and never progress.

**Violence spends anonymity.** A daily ledger A starts at 1.0 when you wake. Each sever: −0.15. Each flare lit: −0.05. Each breaker thrown to I or JORD: −0.05. Each machine killed by grounding: −0.10. Scorches, winch clicks and standoffs: nothing. Floor 0.25. A is the calm ceiling, so after five cuts no bearer that day comes nearer than 4.7 m, and the quiet route has closed behind the loud one until you sleep. Each cut also rouses every wire within 40 m for the rest of the day: head up, watching, reacting to light at 12 m instead of 8 and to sound at 1.5× radius. A noise is counted once per event, not once per listener, and the Collector answers at most one call per 3.6 s, so a flurry of cuts does not compound. How it is shown, never as a number: the bearers stop coming close; below A = 0.7 the tester gains a second, higher tick, the sound of the day's wires awake; one narration line the first day A falls below 0.55: *They kept their distance after that.*; and the hopper next morning, if you were in its country. Sleep or capture resets A to 1.0.

### 3.8 The Collector's bell logic

**Country.** Chapter VI: the facility yard, the scrapyard, the sphere yard, the bicycle forecourt, the culvert mouths outside the fence, and the ice within 300 m of the facility shore, as one polygon; on land the fence and the trunk cable are its edge. Chapter V is not its country, but the ice carries: the hopper bell answers a call at Båten from the facility's bearing at any hour, and it does not move. Chapter VII is not its country and it does not appear. The first call from outside the country gets no answer and the first from inside does; that is how the edge is learned. Its post is the scrapyard, day and night; from the hole in the fence, 180 m off, its tip light is one red point past your beam that might not be a reflector.

**The answer.** Walking is never a call. The call list, inside its country: a cut, a flare lit, a switch or breaker, running on steel or ice, a door pulled shut, a winch click within 12 m, a bell, keying the radio, the radio playing (continuous), the sled's runners over steel (cable trays, grating), the sled hitting steel. 3.6 s after a call it answers: the hopper bell, 262 and 525 Hz in the yard's reverb; then the piston creak 1.5 s before each footfall; then footfalls at 3 s each. It walks toward the call position until its distance is at or under max(0.55 d, 10 m) or it has taken three steps, whichever first, then stops, lamp on, sweeping a 30° cone at 12° per second across the call point, and listens for 40 s. Another call, and 3.6 s later it answers again, closer. Silence for 40 s, and it announces the walk back to its post with the bell and goes. A noise arriving during the 3.6 s wait is not queued: one answer per call. A radio left playing is a continuous call: it comes all the way, lowers the hook and gathers the set, which is the decoy, and channel three is turning every bearer in earshot toward the same set while it does. The bell's first answer in 1997 is its first ever; in 1989 it was Västerskär's post that answered Båten.

**The hook.** At the end of an approach with the call point inside 14 m of the hip beam, the crane swings over it and the hook descends over 4 s with a lamp on it, and closes on anything within 3 m of the call point: crates, a radio, a severed limb, a switched-off bearer, the sled, you. Gathering you is the lift over 8 s into the hopper, a carry like any other, waking on HALL 1's clerk's chair with the sled in the hopper, where by day you can climb its foot and take it back. Being 3 m from where you were loud is enough, so the rule the player learns is: do not stand where you made the noise. The sled is its prey: dragging the sled across a cable tray is a call from the sled's position, and the hook takes the sled if it is within 3 m of the tray; keep it by stopping before the tray, or by letting go of the rope and walking on. The hook cable is the one wire that crosses snow, because it hangs: a live verlet chain with a lamp, and touching it is the wire touch.

**Always seen coming.** It moves only in reply; silent, it stands. Every reply is announced 3.6 s late, and then step by step, so you can count. Its lamp is on whenever it is answering and reads through fog at 120 m. It closes at most 45 percent of the distance per call, so from 60 m it needs three calls to reach you. The hook takes 4 s. It cannot enter a hall: the booms stop at the threshold and only the hook and its cable come in through the door, constrained through the portal point, as a 14 m reach from the doorway, lamp first, so any point deeper than 14 m from an open door is out of reach, and every hall's far end and every hall seat is deeper than 15 m from any portal the cable can enter (a `check-logic` test). A closed door stops the cable, and pulling one shut is a call. Nothing hunts in daylight: in day, dusk and dawn light it does not walk at all; only its bell answers. Being in its lamp does nothing; it is the 1989 searchlight turned inside out, the light that does not see.

## 4. World, levels and systems of place

Three rules from the panel govern this section: hub and spokes across the frozen bay; fixed light per chapter, with days advancing only when you sleep or are carried; and nothing placed behind the player. The bay is the corridor, each site is a dense room, and the farm is the hub because the darkroom is there. Coordinates are metres, x east and z north, origin at the kitchen door of Sjögården. Every site is authored by hand as code (§9); detail (which birch leans, which bicycle has lost its saddle, where a drum froze in) is hashed from position so content can be patched without moving the world. Nothing that moves is seeded.

### 4.1 The bay as a map

The world box is 1.8 km east–west by 2.6 km north–south; the heightfield that carries it is 2048 × 3072 m at 4 m cells with its origin at (−900, −600). The mainland shore runs along the south-west edge. The home island sits in the middle: Sjögården on its south shore facing the bay, Björkhagen inland to the north-west, Näset on its east point, Kärret its north-east marsh. The facility shore closes the bay to the north, with Kyltornen to its west. North-east of the facility the ice thins to an edge and then there is open black water and the lake. The bay is one ice plane; the islands and both shores are heightfield.

The cable is the map's spine. One black cable network leaves the facility, crosses the ice, enters Näset up the village street, runs inland through Björkhagen, crosses the snow field and goes down into the hole where SV-14's slab was. The chapters walk it upstream. Wherever the player is, following the blue toward its source leads north, and following it the other way leads home.

### 4.4 The switchboard grammar

The first game's lighting circuit grown into a system. Vocabulary, all stencilled in Swedish: TAVLA (a board), UTGÅNG (an output), SÄKRING (a fuse), BRYTARE (a breaker), UTTAG (a socket), JORD (earth).

1. A board has three to five outputs. Each output is a corridor: a cable from the board to one consumer (a door, a lift, a lamp circuit, a bell, the radio). A board can carry two outputs at once; a third throws the first. Routing is therefore a choice, never a lock: any output can be reached, only not all together.
2. Throwing an output makes its corridor live. The pulse runs blue along the cable at 4 m/s from the board, so you watch the route light up before anything in it wakes; the consumer works only while its output is on. Wires within 4 m of a live cable wake ten seconds after the pulse passes; bearers on it gain a contact; the hum rises along it. Throw it off and the corridor goes grey from the board outward at the same speed, and a bearer on it has twenty minutes of reservoir before it goes looking (§3.1).
3. Legibility: the route of every output is painted on the board as a line drawing with the lamps and the door marked, and the cable is physically laid along the floor or wall of the corridor and visible from the board. No corridor is ever live that you could not have seen from where you threw it. The tester confirms at your feet.
4. A fuse in a room's breaker box, handle at I, lights that room's lamp circuit for good, independent of any board, and wakes nothing: room lamps are not a light stimulus. Lit rooms are the game's only permanent progression and its only permanent sanctuary: in a lit room wires stay lying and bearers never enter Taking. Six fuses exist; nine boxes accept them (H1, H2, SW-B, J2, the light lock, H3, Båten's galley, the school, Johan's house). You choose which islands get brighter. A fuse adds a 100 Hz tube hum to the room for the rest of the game, the only permanent sound the player adds to the world.
5. JORD, on six boxes only (H1, SW-A, H2, SW-B, J2, J4), grounds that room's floor plates for 30 s and springs back to 0: every machine standing on the floor is Off in five seconds. No door or lift is ever on a floor section's circuit; JORD touches only floors, so grounding is always tactical and never progress. Mätrummet has no box.
6. Sockets are for charging the one battery pool; ten seconds after you plug in, a pulse starts down the nearest cable toward the socket, and when it arrives the corridor is live for a minute (§3.7). You decide how long to stay.
7. Every switch is a call. Calm falls with each throw and the day's wires wake one corridor at a time.

Worked example, HALL 2. TAVLA B has four outputs: the H3 door, the lift L1, the sodium work lamps over the line, the crane. You want the lift. Its cable runs the length of the conveyor along the south wall, where three wires (W6) lie under the benches. Throw L1: the blue runs sixty metres down the wall, ten seconds later the wires lift their ends and look, and the lift hums at the far end. You walk the north gantry in the dark, above the anchor length of the wires, come down S2 at the east end, and ride down. Or throw the lamps instead: the line is lit, the wires under the benches are asleep, but the lift is dead and you go down S1 through HALL 1 and the culverts. Both are ten minutes. HUVUDBRYTARE is separate: it feeds the conveyor. Throw it and the five machines on the line turn their heads to you at once; nothing else happens, ever; they are not finished. It is the game's one switch with no use, the bot proves it is never needed, and a scout stands on its box and the stencil makes it the most inviting switch in the hall so that a third of players do not miss it.

Worked example, the culverts. SW-C has three outputs: the pump at J2, the light lock at J4, and a lamp at J1. The pump drains the flooded section in forty seconds, but its cable runs through J3 where W7 hangs from the ceiling and B6 stands. Wade instead: wet legs, frost creeping from the hem, no call. Or throw the pump and wait behind the sump wall until the wire settles. Or put fuse 4 into J2's box and the junction is lit for good, the wire sleeps, and you wade in light. Three routes, one right for each player.

Worked example, HALL 1. TAVLA A: the door to S1, the desk lamps, the radio. The radio's cable goes out through the wall to the mast. Throw it and channel three turns every bearer in earshot toward the set; you put it down and walk away through the door you powered first.

### 4.5 The ice

Four kinds, legible by colour and by song. Black ice (*kärnis*) is clear and dark, the strongest, and under-ice lights show through it. Grey snow-ice (*stöpis*) is opaque and safe. White ice with a wet dark rim is rotten: the culvert outflow, the walkers' footprints for one night, the edge arc. Blue-lit ice is black ice with a cable under it. The song is a second signal that never lies: thick ice pings low (a 60 Hz boom with a chirp) every 20 to 40 s, and thin ice sings high and often and adds a crack per footfall. The ice-song runs in HRTF so a thin patch is heard to one side before it is seen.

Walking never breaks ice except on rotten patches, which are sung and coloured before you reach them. Running on ice under 8 cm spawns crack decals that propagate from the feet at 1.5 m per step and join; three joined cracks within a 3 m radius is a break-through. Between the second crack and the third there are 1.5 s of sound; stop and it holds. A break-through is real death: the cold fade, no counter, the last seat.

Under-ice lights are a second light list, blue, slow-pulsed along the cable's u, visible through black ice and as a glow through grey; the tester ticks over them. They are the map's spine drawn on the floor. Walker footprints are 4 m holes where a hull stepped, refrozen within a night to black ice with blue in it, thin for one night, then safe. The line of them from the sphere yard to the edge is the ice road of the title.

The sled is a 25 kg box on a 2.5 m rope of eight verlet segments. Friction: 0.35 on snow, 0.20 on boards, 0.06 on ice, 0.03 on wet ice. It lags, swings wide on turns, hisses, and on a down-slope it overtakes you (the rule taught at the ridge: pull, do not be pulled); uphill it drags (speed × 0.7). It carries everything outdoors, stays outside interiors (doors take the rope for you and show the six-slot choice once, with no timer), and is never lost, though the Collector can take it if it was loud in its country. In a sphere's field (`g(r)`) it floats to the rope's length and hangs behind you like a balloon; contents are strapped and do not fall; ice plates under a sphere lift and tilt; snow goes up. A kinematic follow with lag replaces the rope on the phone floor as a ladder rung.

## 5. Narrative, writing and continuity

This section owns every word the player reads: the narration, the cards, the captions, the documents, the registry, the radio and the place-lines; the timeline; the continuity with the first game; and what the sequel is allowed to explain. Everything here is written to be pasted into `text.js` and read by a lint; where a number is stated it is the shipping number.

### 5.1 Style sheet for the narration

The voice is the first game's voice, unchanged, twenty-one instead of thirteen. Only the vocabulary of the job has aged (meter, tester, breaker, line, work order). Rules, in force for every line:

1. **First person, past tense, indicative.** No "I felt", no "I realised", no present tense except inside quoted speech; the album line is the one exempt present.
2. **Two sentences per line.** The second undercuts, widens or quietly contradicts the first. A third sentence is allowed once per chapter, for the chapter's largest fact. Hard cap 24 words; median under 16; a date fragment ("December, 1997.") is exempt from the sentence count.
3. **No fear vocabulary.** Banned: afraid, scared, terrified, terror, fear, dread, horror, horrible, eerie, creepy, sinister, monster, nightmare, scream, panic, ominous, unsettling, strange, weird, unnatural. If a thing is strange, the line states what it did.
4. **Wonder as fact.** No "seemed", "somehow", "impossibly", "as if". The first game spent its one "as if" on 04 looking back.
5. **Adults quoted, never shown, never subtitled.** Roos, Dad, Mom, Uncle Lars, Kerstin exist only as reported speech. No dialogue box anywhere. Documents are rendered as documents in their own hands, never read aloud.
6. **No explanations.** Banned connectives: because, so that, which meant, in order to, the reason, that was why. Two facts may stand next to each other; the narrator never joins them.
7. **Numbers** only as a person says them aloud: years, clock times, "forty seconds", "eleven". Never decimals, never metres.
8. **Small guilt, rationed.** "I told myself" exactly three times (II.9, III.9, V.2). "I should have" once (VIII.9).
9. **Machines and weather share a grammar.** The furthest a machine is personified is "it had somewhere to be".
10. **Never during a threat.** A line fires by position or event, as in the first game, but waits while the tester is ticking, a standoff is live, or the cutter is raised; it fires at the next calm; if the player sleeps first it is dropped. Presentation unchanged: italic Cormorant Garamond at the foot of the frame, one at a time, 3.2 s + length/17 s, a soft gradient beneath. Seats say only their place-line.

Ten lines per chapter; eight ship and two are reserves (r) the level designer may swap in if a trigger point does not exist. The lint (`tools/lint-lines.js`) checks sentence count, word cap, the banned list, no exclamation marks, question marks only inside quotes, the "I told myself" count, 8 to 10 lines per chapter, a minimum trigger spacing of 120 m of path or 90 s of the quiet bot's route, curly apostrophes, a full stop at the end. If the bot shows less than 90 s between triggers the reserves are cut, not squeezed.

### 5.6 What the sequel answers, and what it leaves silent

The rule: **answer with an object anything a child could have found in a drawer; leave silent anything an adult would have to be told.** Answered, only by objects the player can photograph: what the walkers were for and why they stopped (the registry); where the machines went in 1991 (the hulls standing up; Johan's pencil note); who was reading the meter (entry 14); what powers Båten's lamps in the immediate sense (a cable under the boardwalk into the reeds, found in V, never narrated); what the islanders asked for and where they are (the diary; the breath; the loose page); why the bay froze this winter (entry 12); who 04's family is (the L drawer; the cradles).

Left silent, with the mechanism that keeps the silence: **The towers.** Never a word about what they cooled. If Kyltornen is built, its interior has a lake and a half-sunk sphere and no sign, no card, no line beginning "They". VIII.4 says the narrator has stopped asking. **det som svarar.** Never named by the narration, never capitalised, never given a location smaller than "the facility", never a voice; it exists in the diary as a phrase, in the logbook and the card index as a hand with an empty signature column, on the radio as 04's notes an octave down. **04.** No card explains it beyond "lots · går före"; where it goes when it goes up, why it chose this child, whether it is one machine or a series, stay open. **Klotet.** No card at all. **Whether the islanders are content.** The breath says alive; the loose page says warm; the narration never says happy, and the game never shows a face to settle it.

### 5.8 The ending, beat by beat

Chapter VII's last leg begins where the line stops. Hulls stand in a row, one window lit on each, forty metres back from an open lead of black water. Klotet hangs over the water, seams and equator lights, eighteen of them, rotating as in 1989. Hum at 55 Hz. The tester is silent. Painting camera.

1. **The sled lifts** on its rope at eight metres from the sphere's field edge; snow within the field falls upward. VII.6 fires. The player can walk anywhere; the sled floats at hip height and follows.
2. **04.** Two notes from the fog, then it comes out, plain to the eye, hopping and looking back as in 1989. It stops one metre from the player, facing them. VII.8 fires. The prompt *reach out* appears exactly as it has for bearers; the two-second hold; its head turns to the hand; the first game's four-note chord in the original major mode, not the bearers' minor; the tip glows. VII.9 fires. Letting go does nothing here either: it waits and offers again. The cutter cannot be raised inside the field ("the arc would not strike; nothing there would" is the prompt's only feedback). The flash startles it as in 1989 (the higher beep, a hop back, a return); no other effect.
3. **The space on the sled.** 04 hops onto the floating sled and turns to the empty half beside it. VII.10 fires. The equator lights stop rotating. The hum begins to climb from 55 toward 110 Hz over the next 144 seconds. Nothing is listed; no menu; the only prompt on screen is the seat prompt on the sled, identical to every seat in the game.
4. **The tell.** Every 8 seconds one equator light goes dark, starting from the one nearest the player and going round. Visible to the naked eye; centred and countable in the viewfinder, where the breath fogs the eyepiece and clears. Eighteen lights, 144 seconds. Moving more than six metres from the sled pauses the count and 04 turns its head to follow; coming back resumes it. Nothing else in the world moves. If playtests show the tell is missed, the interval halves to 4 s before any prompt is added.

The three outcomes, none named anywhere:

- **Sit.** Sit on the sled. The camera composes the painting: the orange back, the yellow hat, 04 beside, the rope slack. Six seconds; the sled drifts, not up, but back along the line to the nearest hull, whose hatch is open; a bearer's arms take the player in over six slow seconds, camera on the sky, snow on the lens; the window lights. Camera from the bunk: the ice passing under the window; the hull turns away from the shore into the fog. Line: "It was warm. That was the whole idea." Fade. Epilogue B.
- **Walk.** Turn and walk toward the shore. At eight metres the sled leaves the field and drops to the ice with a crack; 04 hops off a moment before and stands. At forty metres, one beep. At sixty, the fog behind the player lights up from within and the player's shadow is thrown long across the ice ahead; the source is never in frame, the rear orbit sees to that. Line: "The fog behind me went the colour of the inside of a shell. I kept walking." Fade at a hundred metres; the shore road; the van; "Roos did not ask anything. Roos started the van." The van seat is the sit that ends it. Epilogue A.
- **Wait.** Stay within six metres, standing, sitting on the ice or looking through the viewfinder, until the last light goes dark. One second of nothing. The beam opens from the sphere to the sled; the sled and 04 rise over nine seconds, hopping, and fade into the sphere as in 1989. Then the hulls: each lifts a metre off the ice with a boom, hangs, and the line rises together over forty seconds into the fog toward the sphere, windows still lit. A flash during this is answered by 04's two notes from above, the last reply in the game. Vantage 48, "It went up", is live for the whole forty seconds. Line: "It went up without looking back. This time, neither did I." Chime; fade; hum back to 55 Hz. Epilogue A.

Falling into the lead is real death and returns the player to the last seat. Being carried cannot happen in VII.

**Epilogue A (walk, wait).** April 1998; the shore road; chapter I's start with the ice gone; Roos's van; the substation; the bay open and empty; the towers silent; the mast dark; the village with no lights across the water; the house for sale. Lines VIII.1 to 8, vantages 46 to 48, the bollard seat, the substation step, then the album.

**Epilogue B (sit).** The same shore-road geometry seen from the bay: the camera is at a hull's window, the hull standing in open water a hundred metres off the shore road, water round its feet, the ice gone. On the road, Roos's van with its hazards on, nobody in frame; the substation; the towers silent. Lines VIII.1, 4 and 7 fire, and the window seat has its line; vantages 46, 47 ("The shore road, from the bay") and 48 ("Roos's van, waiting") are taken from the window and remain thumbnails. Then the album, identical to A's but for one photograph, with the same line. The sit ending is not the sad one and not the good one; the album refuses to score it.

### 5.9 Swedish labels and names

Checked for plausibility, period and collision; a native read of every card and ledger line before ship is still required. **Mälardalens Kraft AB (MDK)** replaces Mellansvenska Kraft, which sits one letter from the real *Mellansvensk Kraftgrupp AB*; the owner should check a register. **Riksvägsverket** is invented, established by the first game's stencil, and now load-bearing: the road agency really did maintain public ice roads, which is why a road agency is the *väghållare* of shelter wagons and why chapter VII is called Isvägen. **skyddsvagn / kraftvagn / bärare / lots** are bureaucratic coinages that read as period; KV also reads as kilovolt beside the "10 kV" box, kept. **Hämtaren** is the definite form of "the one who fetches", with the echo of *sophämtning*, refuse collection. **det som svarar**: always lowercase, never with an article. **Isvägen**, **Islossningen** ("The Ice Going Out"), **Näset**, **Sjögården** (also a common name for community centres, kept), **Björkhagen**, **Mätrummet** (door sign *MÄTRUM · EKOFRITT*), **Kyltornen**, **Fastlandet**, **Anläggningen**, **Klotet**, **Strandstationen**, **Registret**, **Verkstaden**, **Ställverket**, **Handelsboden**: fine. The narrator's job title would be *linjemontör*; the English says line inspector and nothing Swedish states the title. Registry vocabulary (*avställd, väghållare, lev., anm., sign., tillsynsjournal, stängselrond, beredskapsplan, hämtad*) is real administrative Swedish. Names (Roos, Kerstin Sandell, Johan, Ebbe Nilsson, Ruth, Gunnar, the Lindells, the Perssons, the Öbergs, Sigrid Lund, Arne Holm, Elsa Berg) are period-correct for their generations.

### 5.10 How the fiction stays original

The debt to Stålenhag is the landscape, the light and the children-beside-machines composition, acknowledged in the credits line. Never used, in any document, sign, card or line: the names of his books, the Loop or *Slingan*, Riksenergi, the Bona Reactor, magnetrine, Gauss freighters, the neurocaster, Sentre, Mälaröarna as a name, or any of his characters. No flood: our nineties are a freeze. No particle physics, no reactor, no time anomaly, no dinosaurs, no headset; the towers are never explained, which is also the strongest way not to explain them his way. The machines are civil-defence shelter wagons on a 1974 plan with a registry, a road agency and a form that stood them down, and their pathos is that they succeed at their job. The little robot companion is a shared archetype of the genre; ours exists only in a viewfinder, is filed under "lots", and never speaks. The islanders are never seen; their whole presence is breath, a diary, a ledger and a bench with room for a third. The Collector is a specific machine from the first game's own field. The bicycles' reflectors, the meter running backwards, the foil in the windows, the loose page under the thermos, the third hand in the ledger, the darkroom at dawn: made here, and the continuity table in §12 is the proof that each grows out of the first game rather than out of the books.

## 6. Art direction, rendering and photography

### 6.1 The picture we are making

One sentence, so the rest can be checked against it: a gouache of a small orange figure low in a large overcast sky, crisp for the first metre and pulled toward haze by the tenth, with one warm light in it, and no colour that did not come out of a lamp. The first game got there with five parallax canvases pulled toward a haze colour and blurred by distance, a grain pattern and a vignette. The sequel gets there in a forward WebGL2 renderer where every pixel is lit; the lesson in `tech.md` is that the grey look is made by lighting choices in linear light with one shared display transform. Everything below is a fork of grift-city's `render.js` with the city stripped out and the painter's terms added.

### 6.2 The overcast lighting model

The shader keeps grift-city's structure: sRGB-decoded albedo, hemisphere ambient by normal.y, one shadowed directional sun, the 32-light loop with spot cones and per-light Blinn-Phong, emissive from the texture alpha, analytic height fog, then `toDisplay`. What changes is the ratios. The sun's elevation is real (59.5° N: noon on 16 December 7.2°; sunset on the 18th 14:50); each chapter has one fixed sun direction that never moves, and dusk, twilight, fog, night and dawn chapters put the direct term to zero and are lit by the sky gradient and lamps.

On flat snow at noon the direct term is about 4 percent of the light on the ground and 40 percent on a south-facing hull: the low soft sun the panel asked for. The shadow mix is applied as `sh' = mix(1.0, sh, uShadowMix)`, so a fully shadowed patch is never darker than 45 percent of lit; shadows are a tone, not a shape. Two snapped orthographic cascades (near R 18 m centred 10 m ahead, far R 90 m centred 50 m ahead, front-face culled, polygon offset, each culling to its own box), the 3 × 3 PCF replaced by a 12-tap Poisson disc rotated per pixel by a hash (radius 1.5 texels near, 2.5 far). Under a chapter with no sun the sun pass is skipped and its atlas slot goes to the torch. Interiors replace the hemisphere per room: `ambientSky`, `ambientGround` and a `floor` blended over 2 m across a portal; every light carries a room id and touches only its room and adjacent open portals; the cascades are masked to zero inside any roofed room. The sky keeps grift-city's gradient and fbm cloud sheet at coverage 0.92 to 1.0, with a 12:1 horizontally stretched noise at ±3 percent luminance so the gradient never looks digital; the sun disc is off in every chapter except VII, where it is a smear at 8 percent.

### 6.3 Height fog, the haze pull, the distance smear

Height fog is grift-city's analytic integral, tinted toward the sun by `uFogSun` (0.2 in daylight chapters), plus up to four per-volume AABBs summed into the integral (the marsh basin 0.028 / 6 m, a flooded culvert 0.04 / 2 m, HALL 3 0.012 / 12 m). Breath and the hulls' plumes are particles. The haze pull is what the first game did to its far layers, made explicit: the fog colour is the chapter's painted haze, the far is desaturated toward it, `sat = mix(1.0, 0.7, smoothstep(80, 420, d))`, and given paper tooth, a ±3 percent dab noise at 6 px multiplied in with the same weight. It is cheap, always on, and the fallback. The distance smear turns the horizon into gouache and is the riskiest pixel in the game: in the composite, after AO and bloom, `amount = smoothstep(60, 420, d) × 2.1 px`, seven taps along a brush direction (weights 1 2 3 4 3 2 1 / 16, spacing amount/3), the direction horizontal rotated by a static per-48-px-cell hash of ±24° so the paint does not shimmer, a tap accepted only if its depth is ≥ 0.85 × d so a near trunk never bleeds into the bay. About 1.4 ms on an A12 at 1180 × 820; the first rung the ladder sheds; judged on the crossing still beside `?smear=0`, and if it loses the haze pull alone ships and nothing else changes.

### 6.4 Ink line, grain, vignette

grift-city's `edge()` kept at `uEdge` 0.22, thresholds (0.006, 0.020), fading 40 to 160 m: past forty metres the world is paint and paint has no outline. No ink in the viewfinder or in prints. Grain moves into the shader, luminance-weighted (`0.028 + 0.05 × (1 − lum)`), 1.6× in the viewfinder, clumped at 2 px in prints, and frozen rather than removed under reduced motion, because without it the dark reads as a video game. Vignette 0.16 on the display-referred colour; the viewfinder replaces it with a circular eyepiece vignette to 0.35 with a hard rim at the 3:2 frame. Grain and vignette live in `DISPLAY_GLSL`, so the post-off rung still looks like the game.

### 6.5 The colour law in the grade

Three enforcements. Pigment is muted, light is saturated: painted albedos stay below chroma 0.20 except the parka #c9542a, the hat #e3be45, the machines' stripe #d7782f and hazard stripes #d9892f; Falu red is painted #7b2d22 and lands at chroma 0.17, a brown-red, never a red light. Emissives are painted only in the three law colours (#7fe0ff, #ff5a3c, #ffb65c), a flare's warm white, and the CRT's phosphor at low intensity. A hue-gated saturation in `gradeDisplay`: after the S-curve and the warm-light/cool-shade split at 0.6 strength, `w = 1` within ±18° of 200° (blue), ±14° of 10° (red, which covers the parka), ±16° of 38° (amber), else 0.55; `sat *= mix(0.72, 1.0, w)` where chroma > 0.08. A lint: one 1280 × 720 frame per chapter and per room at rung 0 must have under 1.5 percent of pixels with chroma > 0.25 outside the gates. Warm lights are the only lights allowed to be soft: sodium and the torch bloom at 1.0, blue seams 0.35, red LEDs 0.0. A red light never blooms on screen; it halates only on paper.

### 6.6 Exposure, dark adaptation, the torch, the beam

Adaptation without a readback: mipmaps of the resolved RGBA16F scene; a 1 × 1 ping-pong target reads 36 taps from mip 5, weighted to the centre 60 percent, each soft-clipped to 8 × key so the torch's hotspot does not drive the meter, and lerps toward `key / logAverage` with τ 0.35 s when the scene brightens and 6 s when it darkens (full dark adaptation about eighteen seconds). Keys: 0.42 daylight, 0.26 dusk, afterglow and dawn, 0.14 night and the facility. `uExposure` clamped to [0.8, 7.0]; ambient floors added in the lit shader (0.0035 linear in the facility at night, 0.012 outdoors at night, 0 by day); above exposure 3 a Purkinje shift, `c = mix(c, lum × (0.85, 0.95, 1.10), 0.25 × smoothstep(3, 7, exposure))`, so the dark is blue-grey and the only reds in the frame are the ones that emit. High contrast (a setting) raises the floors by 0.6 EV. The luminance test: per room and chapter, torch off and on, display-referred p10 ≥ 0.035 and p50 ≥ 0.12 with the torch on, p10 ≥ 0.02 with it off, at rungs 0 and 6, on the iPad; a failure raises that room's floor, never lowers the standard; it runs from the first build.

The torch: a spot bound to the head bone with a 120 ms slerp lag; cone 16°/26°; 3300 K, linear (1.0, 0.67, 0.39); intensity 45 at one metre, quadratic falloff, radius 22 m; 2 percent flicker at 1 Hz under 15 percent battery. Its shadow: a perspective slot in the existing DEPTH_COMPONENT24 atlas sampled by the same `sampler2DShadow` PCF; laptops 4096 × 2048 (sun near 2048², far 1024², torch 1024², lamp 1024²), iPad 2048 × 2048 (1024², 512², 1024², 512²), the torch taking 2048² when the sun pass is off. The lamp slot goes to the nearest of a sodium lamp within 20 m, a bearer's work lamp when it comes on, the Collector's lamp, the safelight. The beam: a half-res RGBA16F pass, the view ray intersected with the cone analytically, 24 steps with interleaved-gradient-noise offsets, visibility from a single-compare `shadow1()` (never the 12-tap PCF, which would cost 288 taps per pixel), cone falloff, inverse-square, Henyey-Greenstein g 0.55, density `σ = σ_chapter + 0.6 × noise3D(p × 0.35 + wind × t) + breath + snowfall` from a 32³ R8 `texStorage3D` painted at boot; σ_chapter 0.010 indoors, 0.018 in snowfall, 0.045 in Kärret, 0.004 in Mätrummet; upsampled depth-aware and added before bloom; snow sprites sample the same cone. Target 3.5 ms on the iPad. A pillar cutting a shaft out of the beam is the horror's cheapest silhouette, which is why the shadow is rendered before the beam.

### 6.7 The adaptive quality ladder

`autoStep` unchanged (an EMA of frame time, 2.5 s slow before lowering, 20 s fast before raising, the 30 Hz cap probe, a count of raises that did not hold). Rung 0: everything, at dpr 1 on any touch WebKit device or any device with a detected 30 Hz cap, and dpr up to 2 on desktop only. Rungs: 1 smear off, bloom at eighth res; 2 beam 24 → 12 steps with a bilateral, AO off; 3 render scale 0.85, lamp shadow off; 4 beam replaced by two tapered cone quads, MSAA 4× → 2×; 5 render scale 0.7, ink off, far cascade off; 6 torch shadow 1024² → 512², sun shadows off; 7 post off through `DISPLAY_GLSL`. ### 6.8 The painted-materials pipeline

grift-city's `textures.js` framework kept (a layer registry, RGBA8 `sampler2DArray` colour with emissive in alpha written through ImageData, a parallel normal+roughness array, derivative tangent frames, mipmaps with anisotropy, an IndexedDB deflate cache keyed by a hash of the painter source, canvases released after upload); its photographic bases go. Foil is a crinkle field of 3 px strokes on #d9dade painted into the height at 3.5 so the specular breaks into facets, roughness 0.2; black ice is long drags at 0.05 alpha over #0e1a18 with roughness 0.08 and a crack layer in the emissive band for the under-ice lights; the stencil pass paints block letters stroke by stroke. Budgets: laptop 512² (about 34 MB GPU, 9 MB cache; 1024² for snow and concrete only if the M3 look test asks); iPad 256² (about 9 MB). The material bench `?bench=name` shows a sphere, a cube, a plane and a bent pipe under the eight chapter lights and the torch; every material passes under Isen, Anläggningen and the torch before it goes into a level, and each screenshot is a human gate written into the log.

### 6.10 The painting camera and the viewfinder

## 7. Audio

Everything in Stålhagen II is heard before it is seen, and a bot tests it. Quiet means safe. Nothing is louder than a walker's footfall. Every sound is WebAudio, generated at load or at the moment it plays; no samples, no voices, no stingers. The first game's `audio.js` is the seed: its chord tables, its 55 Hz sine and 110.4 Hz triangle hum, its 3.6 s and 4.8 s delays and its four-note touch chord are continued exactly and given a third dimension.

### 7.1 The sound law

1. **Announced before seen**, per threat type (§7.8), asserted by the encounter-timing test on every bot run by both routes.
2. **The tester ticks before anything moves.** The sim wakes a machine 2.0 s after charge reaches its contact, and starts a wire's approach 2.0 s before its head rises; audio needs no special code for this.
3. **Nothing louder than a walker's footfall.** A walker's foot landing 30 m away is the mix's 0 dB reference and peaks at −6 dBFS.
4. **Replies are quieter than footsteps and never loop.**
5. **The score does not react to danger.** The pad follows chapter and place; the hum is the only instrument that follows threat; the one exception, the pad going silent in Mätrummet, is a reaction to a place.
6. **No stingers.** The developed print that shows one more bearer is silent. The only chord attached to an event is the touch chord, and the player causes it.
7. **An audio colour law.** The 55 Hz family (55 Hz sine, 110.4 Hz triangle, their beat) is the charge, and is blue. The 100 Hz family (ballasts, transformers, tubes) is human electricity, and is sodium-yellow: a fuse in a room gives it a 100 Hz hum, never 55. Red, the thing that watches, has no sound of its own except the servo tick of a head re-aiming.

### 7.2 The graph

One AudioContext, created on the first input event, resumed on `visibilitychange`, `latencyHint` interactive. Buses: `master` (0.8) → compressor (−16 dB, ratio 3, knee 12, attack 4 ms, release 180 ms) → destination; under master: `pad` (the score) with a room send of 0.6; `world` (12 HRTF panners and 8 equalpower ring sectors) with a room send per source of 0.2 to 0.5; `sub` (non-positional, lowpass 90 Hz: walker feet, booms, the hum's sine); `beds` (stereo, no panner: wind, the hum's triangle, tester, heartbeat, radio carrier); `foley` (the player's own body, dry); `ui`; and `room`, two ConvolverNodes crossfaded over 400 ms on room change. The sub bus exists because HRTF is meaningless below about 100 Hz and a panner costs the same at 30 Hz as at 3 kHz. Continuous nodes are built once and driven with `setTargetAtTime`; transient voices are created per event and capped at 48; the 49th is dropped. In `__sim` mode there is no AudioContext: every call goes to a recorder that logs {t, kind, x, y, z, level} so the headless bot can assert lead times.

### 7.3 The pad, the hum, the tester

The pad is the first game's synthesis unchanged: five-note chords every 9 s, 10 s long, triangle root and sawtooths above, doubled at −7 and +6 cents with a 4-cent scatter, lowpass 820 Hz (520 in the dark chapters) with a 0.13 Hz LFO, bells from the current chord at three sine partials into the reverb every 3 to 10 s, wind as bandpassed noise wandering 350 to 850 Hz (250 to 600 in April). Chord tables in MIDI: I the snow field's set with the thirds opened, [45,52,57,59,64] [41,48,55,60,64] [36,48,55,59,62] [43,50,57,59,62], wind 0.30; II and VIII the epilogue's set, [36,48,55,60,64] [41,53,57,60,64] [33,45,52,57,60] [43,50,55,59,62], wind 0.14; III D minor, [38,50,57,62,65] [46,53,58,62,65] [41,53,57,60,65] [45,52,57,60,64], wind 0.18; IV August's set with every major third lowered, [38,50,57,62,65] [43,50,59,62,65] [40,52,59,64,67] [45,52,57,60,64], wind 0.12; V the marsh's set unchanged at lowpass 520, [40,47,52,55,59] [36,48,52,55,59] [38,50,54,57,62] [35,47,50,54,59], wind 0.20; VI the facility's set at 520, 3 dB down, bells every 8 to 20 s, [36,43,48,51,58] [32,44,48,51,55] [34,41,46,50,53] [31,43,47,50,55], wind 0.22 outside and 0 inside except at portals; VII Klotet's dawn set, [41,53,57,60,65] [38,50,57,62,65] [46,53,58,62,65] [48,55,60,64,67], wind 0.16; Kyltornen VI down five semitones at 400. The pad sits at −26 dB relative to the footfall reference, never rises for danger, rises 2 dB when the player sits, and fades over 3 s on sleep.

The hum is the instrument of proximity to charged steel. The current graph publishes the charged elements; each frame audio takes d to the nearest and `p = clamp(1 − (d − 2) / 26, 0, 1)`, zero at 28 m and one at 2 m. The sine's frequency is `55 + 55 p²`; its level `FLOOR[chapter] + 0.14 p` with floors [0, 0.01, 0.01, 0.02, 0.02, 0.06, 0.03, 0] (Kyltornen 0.05); time constants 0.6 s up, 2.5 s down, so leaving is a decision you hear yourself make. The triangle stays at 110.4 Hz; when the sine reaches 110 they beat at 0.4 Hz, a 2.5 s wobble that exists only within about 3 m of live steel: the within-reach signal of the standoff, free (retuned to 110.2 Hz if the listening test hears a bug). A pulse within 6 m swells the gain 3 dB by its phase. The sine goes to the sub bus, the triangle to beds; on a phone speaker only the triangle is heard, which is enough. The hum is not positional: charge is under the floor and everywhere. `humCeiling` is 1, or 0.3 under quiet sound.

The tester: rate `18 p²` ticks per second plus a burst of six when a pulse passes within 6 m, silent at p = 0; below anonymity 0.7 a second, higher tick. A tick is a 2 ms highpassed click at 4 kHz and a 6 ms sine at 3.3 kHz at −26 dB on the beds bus through a fixed StereoPanner at +0.3 (it hangs on the right hip), never HRTF, never muted by quiet sound. Because the sim wakes machines 2.0 s after charge arrives, the tester has always been ticking two seconds before the first movement.

### 7.4 Mätrummet

On crossing its portal, over 1.5 s: hum to 0, both room sends to 0, pad to 0, wind to 0. What remains: the player's own foley, dry, with a 3 dB high shelf at 4 kHz so the room sounds dead rather than quiet; the tester; and the heartbeat, which exists nowhere else: two thumps 130 ms apart (a 48 Hz sine for 90 ms, a 62 Hz sine for 70 ms, lowpass 120 Hz) at 58 bpm at full calm and 96 at zero, on beds at −24 dB. The five in their ring make no sound but a servo tick when a head turns to follow you. The sphere is silent. 04 beeps from where it stands. Leaving reverses the fade over 2.5 s, and the hum comes back before the reverb does.

### 7.5 Space

The listener sits at the player's head, oriented by the camera (camera forward, so behind you means behind the screen; the viewfinder needs no special case). Twelve PannerNodes (HRTF, inverse, refDistance 1, rolloff 1, maxDistance 400) created once and reassigned; eight equalpower panners fixed on a 60 m ring at 45° steps as sectors for everything else. Every positional thing registers {x, y, z, importance, reach, node, swarm}; score = importance / (1 + d / reach); every 100 ms the registry sorts and the top twelve non-swarm sources hold the panners with 20 percent hysteresis and 250 ms crossfades through two gains per source, so no running node is reconnected mid-sound. Swarms (the heads) are one virtual source at the centroid with a width of ring sectors. Importances: the Collector 10, 04 9, a bearer 8, a wire's live end 6, the radio 6, a bell 6, a walker's mid component 5, a small sphere 5, a scout 4, the sled 4, a crack 4, a bird 2, the train 1; the sled 1.5 m behind you always wins a slot, because the sled is company. Cross-room sources are placed at the first portal on a breadth-first path (at most four hops) with path-length distance, a 4 kHz lowpass per open door, 1.2 kHz and −9 dB per closed door, full occlusion at 300 Hz and −20 dB; outdoors `raycast3` decides obstruction (2 kHz, −6 dB); two BiquadFilters per source. The same search, computed once per noise per room, is what the creatures' hearing uses (§3.1).

### 7.6 Rooms

Every room profile names an impulse response synthesised on first entry in the worker (pre-generated with the chapter's paint) and kept in a cache of twelve: pre-delay; six first-order early reflections at the room's dimensions with alternating sign per channel; a three-band noise tail with its own RT60 per band; an optional metallic comb at 180 Hz; an optional flutter at 12.8 ms for tubes. Profiles (RT60 low/mid/high in seconds; dimensions in metres): HALL 1 2.6/3.2/1.4, 18 × 7 × 30, pre 12 ms; HALL 2 2.4/2.8/1.2, 24 × 9 × 40, metal 0.2; HALL 3 3.4/3.8/1.6, 30 × 14 × 80, the longest tail in the game; a culvert 1.6/2.2/0.6, 2.2 × 2.2 × 60, flutter 0.35; a kitchen 0.5/0.45/0.3; the darkroom 0.3/0.25/0.15; the bus shelter 0.3/0.25/0.2 with four early reflections; Båten's interior 1.2/1.4/0.8, metal 0.3; a hull's bunk 0.8/0.9/0.5, metal 0.25; the facility yard as outdoors with two images off hall walls at 60 and 120 ms at −14 dB and a 0.9 s tail at −22; the birches a 0.4 s scatter at −18; open ice no tail, one ground image at 6 ms and a far-shore return at 1.1 s at −30 dB through 800 Hz, so a shout on the bay comes back once, a second later, from the mainland; Mätrummet no IR. A 3.8 s stereo IR at 48 kHz is 1.46 MB; twelve are under 12 MB.

### 7.7 The ice, the footfall bus, the voices

A crack far away arrives as a descending chirp because a plate is dispersive, and that is the synthesis. The crack: 12 ms of highpassed noise at 3 kHz. The boom: a sine from 60 + 20 × energy Hz falling to 55 percent over 0.6 s on the sub bus, plus 0.3 s of lowpassed noise at 220 Hz, positional. The song: a sine sweeping 2400 to 160 Hz over `0.25 + min(1.4, d / 220)` s with a second partial at 1.31× and 35 percent level, into the open-ice IR; thin ice starts at 3200, ends at 420 and is 40 percent as long, from under your feet. Energies: a walking step 0.15, running 0.35, the sled 0.1, a walker's foot 1. A step on ice of thickness T rolls a crack with probability `clamp((0.12 − T) / 0.12, 0, 0.8)`, × 0.3 walking, × 1 running; over thick ice the bay sings on its own from 40 to 300 m every 20 to 60 s by day and every 10 to 30 s in VII. A stone slid on black ice plays the song from the stone with f0 2000 Hz for as long as it slides.

Every foot lands through one function, `footfall({x, y, z, surface, mass, actor, run, room})`, and four systems read it: audio, the hearing list (radius = surface base × √mass, doubled running), decals and calm. Masses: scout 0.15, player 1, bearer 3, the Collector 40, a walker 120. Surfaces: snow (a 1.4 to 2 kHz burst plus a 4 kHz tick; a crust crunch at 600 Hz in IV and VII), ice (a 3 kHz click and a ring), plank (a 420 Hz knock through a resonant bandpass, hollow), steel grating, hull plate, concrete, gravel, marsh, kitchen wood, tile. The walker's footfall is five parts on a six-second period: knee hydraulics 1.5 s before (noise sweeping 300 to 900 Hz, positional at the knee); the sub (42 to 28 Hz over 1.2 s, attenuated by 1 / (1 + d / 60)); the impact (0.35 s of noise at 180 Hz, positional); the ice response at energy 1; 0.8 s later the settle (a 38 Hz sawtooth through 120 Hz for 0.9 s). At 30 m the sum peaks at −6 dBFS; at 300 m the sub is still audible at −30 dB and everything else is gone. A gamepad footfall rumble of 40 ms at 0.6 within 60 m, off in quiet sound. The sled's runner hiss is bandpassed noise at 900 Hz over snow and a 2.6 kHz whisper over ice, following rope tension × speed, silent when it floats; its stopping is a tell.

Voices. 04 unchanged (1320/1760 Hz squares, importance 9, positional even though only the viewfinder draws it). Scouts: the same notes, hash-detuned ±3 to 40 cents, 30 percent dropping the second note. Channel one is Roos as a murmur: a sawtooth at 110 to 140 Hz with vibrato through two bandpass formants walking randomly (F1 300 to 700 Hz, F2 900 to 2200 Hz, Q 4), a syllable envelope at 3 to 5 Hz in phrases of 0.8 to 2.4 s, band-limited 350 to 2800 Hz under the carrier at −30 dB; the no-words test decides whether it survives and the fallback is carrier plus text. The darkroom: the safelight's transformer at 100 Hz with faint partials (−40 dB), a drip into the tray every 2 to 9 s with a 480 Hz tank resonance, the tray rocking, a wet sweep of 0.4 s when the print lifts; no tick. The house: the kitchen IR, the fridge at 100 Hz. A fuse: the clunk, three tube strikes, a 100 Hz hum at −40 dB that stays for the rest of the game. Quiet encounters keep their 1989 sounds where they recur; bullfinches are soft descending sines from 2.2 kHz; the swing a chain creak every 1.6 s while it moves; the train's two-note horn every ninety seconds through the open-ice return.

### 7.8 Threats announced

Minimum lead from first audible cue to first possible visible movement, asserted on both bot routes; fog visibility in V is 25 m and a beep carries 60 m: scout, two notes at 60 m, harmless, no assertion; bearer, two notes at 45 m or more and at least two beeps before it can be within 25 m, the servo tick whenever it re-aims, lead 8 s at walking pace; wire, the drag hiss 2.0 s before the live end can move; walker, knee hydraulics 1.5 s before every footfall and the sub audible at 300 m; KV-3 standing, the groan 4 s before motion; the heads, the signal sound 1.7 s before the first lights and 1.1 s between; a small sphere, the shimmer at 14 m, 6 m of walking before the field; the Collector, the bell 3.6 s after your call, then footfalls at 3 s, then the chain at the door; the workshop line, clunk, 0.5 s, five ticks; any machine waking from charge, the tester at 2.0 s. The tester clause exempts walkers, the heads and the Collector, whose announcements are the under-ice light, the signal sound and the bell.

### 7.9 Mixing, quiet sound, budget, tests

Levels relative to the walker footfall at 30 m: a near crack-and-boom −2; the arc −4 (0.5 s, the first game's zap); a flare igniting −6, burning as a bed at −24; the player's steps −14 to −10; a beep at 10 m −16, at 45 m −30; score bells −24; answer bells −28; the pad −26 (−29 in VI); wind −22 outdoors (−18 on the crossing); the hum −36 to −18 (the facility floor −24); the tester −26; the sled −30; the radio carrier −26 and the murmur −30; the heartbeat −24; UI ticks −30. Ducking by gain automation: the touch chord ducks everything but wind 9 dB for 4 s; a walker footfall within 40 m ducks the pad 6 dB for 1.5 s; the shutter ducks the beds 12 dB for 80 ms; in the viewfinder the pad is 3 dB down and the world 2 dB up; when the player sits the hum sinks to the chapter floor over 4 s and the pad rises 2 dB; sleep fades all but the bells over 3 s. Narration is text and ducks nothing. Quiet sound (a setting, distinct from quiet mode; both default on together on phones) halves the sub, caps the hum at 70 Hz and −28 dB, soft-clips crack transients at −14 dB, drops the rumble, and keeps every announcement, the tester and the Collector's bell. A headphones/speakers toggle switches the twelve panners between HRTF and equalpower, default HRTF, stated on the settings screen.

Budget: 15 percent of one core on a 2019 iPad. Rungs, in order: IR length 4 s to 2.5 s; HRTF slots 12 to 8; ring sectors 8 to 4; HRTF to 6. Events are stamped with sim time and scheduled at `ac.currentTime` plus 50 ms lookahead, so footfalls land where the foot did; nothing sets `.value` on a running node; the sample rate is read, never assumed. The diegetic-pad experiment: each of the five voices anchored to the nearest cable pulse or contact in earshot, breathing with its pulse phase, routed through ring sectors (no panner cost), a severed contact killing its voice for good; prototyped in the first culvert only; go requires four of six listeners to say the music changed when they cut, four of six to place the chord in the pipes, nobody to say broken, no preference for the plain pad by more than one point; otherwise the plain pad stands and a severed contact still removes that machine's hum contribution, which the hum system does for free.

Bench tests, headless with OfflineAudioContext: each IR's RT60 per band within 10 percent; the hum at 28, 10, 4 and 2 m at 55, 61, 92 and 110 Hz within 1 Hz with the beat present only at 2 m; every bot run with no sample above −1 dBFS, the walker footfall the loudest event, every reply below the player's step; the lead table from the `__sim` recorder. Human tests, six listeners on headphones, blind, at the slice and at month six: localisation (a beep from eight bearings; pass 75 percent front-back correct; fallback a 3 dB high-shelf cut for rear sources); fog ambiguity (bearer, scout and 04 at 30 m indistinguishable, distinguishable at 10); thin ice (two paths by sound alone with the screen black; five of six name the thin one); the standoff (rated 1, a prompt, to 5, a held breath; pass a median of 4); the murmur (no word from any listener); quiet sound (the same thresholds); the diegetic pad's go/no-go.

## 8. Pacing, contemplation, controls and accessibility

This section spends the charter (§10): a register curve for the eight chapters, the quiet encounters with their timings, the two rest verbs, the control schemes for three input kinds, the settings, and a deliberate attack on the one risk every judge raised, that six hours with nobody in it will feel empty. Vocabulary: a *call* is any act of power or noise the player makes; *calm* is §3.3's value; a *threat* is a placed machine or wire whose behaviour can end in a carry or a death; *light* is a per-chapter enum, `day | dusk | night`, set when a chapter card plays and never changed by a clock (the afterglow and dawn are `dusk` for the hunting rule; fog is `day`).

### 8.1 The curve

Four registers, tagged per minute of the quiet bot's route: **quiet** (walking, looking, kitchens; no tester tick and no spectacle), **wonder** (a spectacle or a reply; nothing that can carry you), **fear** (a threat inside tester range), **rest** (sitting, sleeping, reading, the darkroom).

Fear is 21 percent of the core and never more than 41 percent of any chapter. Three shape rules are asserted on the bot log: no fear run longer than 9 minutes without a seat reachable inside 60 s; no quiet run longer than 12 minutes without a wonder beat or a reply; and the first two hours contain exactly one bearer. Threats by hour: hour one, one (the porch wire at minute 40); hour two, two; hour three, six; hour four, nine; the last hour, none. The peak is one threat every eight minutes in the facility; denser than that and the walk becomes a corridor.

**B1, the field bearer, chapter III, minutes 85 to 93.** It stands at the far end of the pitch, 80 m off, lamp off, indistinguishable from a scout, on a live cable so the tester ticks from 28 m. At 25 m in daylight it notices you if you are still: a 2.0 s look, then the lamp comes on, and it walks at 0.35 m/s, pausing every six seconds to lift its head and sound the two notes. Walk, and it stops where it is; that is the first thing it teaches, before anything else, so that "nothing hunts" is learned where everything can be seen. Stand, and calm fills in seventeen seconds and it walks the rest of the way, about seventy seconds from 25 m, and stops at 0.8 m, facing you, breathing. Then the prompt. The whole first standoff, from the moment you stop to the moment the LED goes dark red, is about ninety seconds of standing still while a machine walks toward you, on a football pitch, at noon, with the towers behind it. That is the scene the slice reproduces in the afterglow, and the scariest thing the game does, and it happens in daylight. The standoff has three honest exits, reach out, walk slowly away, or sit down, and every one of them costs patience, which is the resource this game trades in.

### 8.2 The quiet encounters

Twenty-six in the core (the first game had six). Every one keeps the first game's three laws: nothing gates the journey, the reply comes after enough silence to wonder, no score. Every machine in earshot turns its head to watch a reply, and a bearer's head turning to a bullfinch is the same head-turn it gives your hand. Timings are asserted by `check-life.js`.

- **The radio** (§5.4): the key is a call; every reply is 4.8 s; channel three turns every bearer within 40 m to the set for 20 s. Keying draws nothing from the battery, because it is sound, not light.
- **Bullfinches**: on the rowan by the bus shelter, at the feeder outside Johan's kitchen, on the sled rope on the last morning. Stand within 6 m at under 0.2 m/s for 3.5 s and they settle beside you; run within 4 m and they scatter for 8 s; the interact key near a nest is a soft whistle that settles them at once. They are the one red thing on the islands that is not a light, painted exactly as red as an LED so the eye at distance cannot tell.
- **Stones on black ice**: a pile at the shore by the substation; the sled carries five. Slide one and it travels 20 to 40 m and the ice sings; thin ice sings higher and shorter, so the stone is also the honest way to read the ice ahead. 2.4 s after the stone stops the ice answers with the same song from far off; once, on the bay in V, from under.
- **The bell** on Båten's post: 3.6 s later a lower bell from Västerskär, and 3.6 s after that the hopper bell from the facility's bearing, lowpassed through 500 Hz, pitched down. In the facility yard the 1974 muster bell hangs on the gate post; ring it at night and 3.6 s later the Collector answers, closer, which is where a quiet encounter becomes a tool. In VII a hull's bell rings once as you pass and nothing answers it.
- **Lighting circuits**: the first game's lever box; lamps come on 0.45 s apart with the 110 Hz hum and go off in reverse. In Näset the village lamps are seven; the seventh never lights, and 4.8 s after the sixth a red LED comes on in the dark shop window instead.
- **The swing**: two in the schoolyard; the near one is a seat. Push off and after three periods the second swing begins to move in time with yours, its amplitude matching yours with a 3.6 s lag, and it stops 3.6 s after you do. A scout, if present, watches the empty one.
- **The walker's stove and window** (§3.2): from the bunk, the stove takes kindling from the crate and forty seconds to catch (a match, not battery); warmth restores the frost from your hem and the smoke goes out the vent as the same plume every docked hull breathes, so for once the breath is yours.
- **Lying down in the snow**: hold the sit key 1.2 s anywhere on snow. The camera goes to the sky, flakes land on the lens, breath rises, and within 8 m of a sphere the flakes go the other way and you drift a hand's breadth off the snow. Lying down is absolute and does not save. Frost creeps in after 60 s; you stand up cold, and any heat restores you. Lie still 20 s with the camera raised and 04's head comes into the top of the frame, looking down.
- **04 comes back**: stand still 5 s and 04 beeps from where it waits and comes into frame within 12 s to see why, whether or not the camera is raised; it is only seen if it is. The cheapest company the game has.
- The remaining seventeen are texture on the same rules: the train's horn answered by the far-shore return; the fridge at the farm; the feeder; the shop's door bell; the hull's bell; the school's tied-up rope; the drums lifting under the sphere; the pebbles by the sphere yard; the culvert's warm water; the reflectors; the meter; the crow's nest's view; the birds' nest in KV-3's gearbox; the Advent star's circuit; the harbour pump; the Volvo's horn (it still works, once); the tractor's seat.

### 8.3 Sit and sleep

**Sit.** The context key within 1.2 m of a seat, facing it within 90°. The body turns and sits over 1.1 s; the save happens at the instant of contact, silently, with no icon (the pause screen says *Sitting down keeps your place*). The camera pulls back over 2.5 s to the shot stored for that seat; you may orbit ±25° inside it. If the seat has a line and it has not been said, it appears 1.5 s after the camera settles. While sitting you may look, raise the camera and photograph, key the radio, and turn the torch on, since sitting is absolute regardless of light. Sitting freezes calm (no farming from a bench) and does not remove a threat: an engaged bearer waits at 3 m. Standing takes 0.9 s on any movement input and the camera returns over 1.5 s. The seats are §5.5's; the sled is a seat anywhere outdoors: it saves, composes a generic wide shot, and says its one line once.

**Sleep.** At beds only: the farm, Johan's room, Båten's bunk, a docked hull in HALL 3. The context key shows *sleep*; a 1.5 s hold, because sleeping is the one irreversible verb; the sleep prompt's second line reads *Nights move the world. Sleep when you are ready.* A 2.4 s fade, the bed's line if it has one, then the next day's card if a chapter card is due. Waking: the same bed, the sled where you left it, warmth restored, the anonymity ceiling restored, the battery charged only if the bed is on a live socket (the farm, Båten and the hull are; Johan's room is not), and the morning shot composed to show what the night cost. Any bed spends a night and applies the table; light changes only when a chapter card plays.

### 8.4 Controls

One input path: touch and gamepad both emulate keys and a virtual pad, as in grift-city. Verbs: walk; hurry (§3's run speeds; costs calm at −1 per second and is a call on ice and steel); look; context (interact, sit, reach out, read, take the rope, climb); torch; raise the tool in hand; use the raised tool; cycle the tool (camera, cutter, winch, flare); radio key and dial; let go of the rope; pause; album. There is no crouch key: the capsule ducks under low ceilings and through the hatch by itself, and stillness is the only stealth posture. There is no jump.

**Keyboard and mouse.** WASD walk (arrows always work, unrebindable); Shift hurry; mouse look; E context; Q torch; right mouse raise (hold by default); left mouse use; wheel or 1 to 4 cycle the tool; R radio (press to key, hold to dial, one click per 0.4 s); G let go of the rope; Space reach out (a second binding of context, so the prompt can say *space* to a player whose E is under a finger); F the flash toggle; Esc pause; Tab album; M mute. Pointer lock with a fallback to free-mouse deltas.

**Gamepad.** Left stick walk, push to the rim to hurry; right stick look; A context and reach out; X torch; LT raise; RT use; LB and RB cycle; Y radio; B let go; D-pad down flash; Start pause; Back album. Rumble: a 30 ms pulse per tester tick scaled by rate (suspended during the hold), 40 ms at 0.6 per walker footfall within 60 m, one 40 ms pulse at the twitch.

**Touch.** A floating stick under the left thumb; a drag anywhere on the right half looks; the buttons show only what the moment needs. Always: CONTEXT (large, lower right), TORCH, RAISE (a toggle on touch), PAUSE. When raised: the context button becomes SHUTTER, CUT, HOOK or THROW by the tool in hand, and a four-icon tray above it picks the tool. Within 3 m of the sled or at a doorway: ROPE. In the standoff: the context button reads REACH and grows, and its release has an 80 ms dead zone, which under D5 costs nothing either way. RADIO top-left. Buttons scale and can move to the left hand.

**Reach out.** When a bearer stands at one metre facing you with its lamp on, the prompt says *reach out*. Hold the context key (or Space, A, REACH) for the two seconds of §3.5; release at any time withdraws the hand and nothing happens. A flinch is sustained movement over 120 ms above a 0.35 deadzone during the hold, or hurry, torch on, the flash, the cutter raised or a flare lit at any moment inside its reach; the bearer lifts you over six seconds, and you can cut the shoulder seam during those seconds or let it. Looking around, raising the camera without the flash, and the shutter are not flinches. There is no timer on the standoff. A setting makes reach out a press-to-start, press-to-withdraw toggle, under which the flinch set is only the active inputs, exactly as under the hold; on worn controllers a second setting ignores the movement stick entirely while the reach is held.

### 8.5 Settings and accessibility

Native form elements in a dialog, saved on change, all available from the title screen. Defaults per device: on phones (shortest side under 600 px) quiet mode, larger text and reduced motion are on, and the title screen says so in one line.

- **Quiet mode.** Bearers and the Collector never lift; a flinch makes a bearer step back 1 m and watch, and calm resets. Walkers hold their step for any player within 4 m. A wire's drag stops at 0.6 L. Thin ice, falls and live anchors remain, since those are the player's own steps. Nights therefore advance only by sleeping. Switchable at any time; nothing in the album, the endings or the logbook records it.
- **Larger text.** Narration, captions, documents, cards and menus at 1.35×; the lint renders every document at this size and fails on overflow.
- **Reduced motion.** The painting camera does not drift; it cuts to the composition through 0.4 s of black on sit. Snow density halved; grain frozen, not removed; the workshop lamps' flicker steadied; the walker footfall's shake removed; the carry's sky camera does not turn. The upward snow near a sphere is kept, sparser.
- **Colour law, safe palettes.** The three law colours are one table of three linear RGB constants read by the grade, every emissive palette, the caption colours and the album borders. Presets: default; protan/deutan (red toward magenta, sodium toward white-warm); tritan (blue toward cyan-white, warmth toward orange-red). Independently of palette the law has three signatures that are not colour: red always blinks, blue always flows, warm light always holds steady. `tools/check-palette.js` simulates each preset through the Machado 2009 matrices and requires a CIEDE2000 distance of at least 20 between every pair of law colours.
- **High contrast.** Raises the ambient floors by 0.6 EV and thickens the ink line; the luminance test still governs the floor for everyone.
- **Captions.** Off by default; on, a caption band and a thin bearing arc at the screen's edge in the direction of the sound, weight for distance. Emitted from the same event bus as audio at the same timestamp; the vocabulary before a machine type is seen: *two notes, far, left*; *a footfall, near*; *the tester ticks*; *the ice sings, thin*; *something lifts its head*. After: *a bearer, two notes, near*. Captions never precede their sound and never say more than HRTF would.
- **The tester in the hands and the eye.** A belt LED lights with each tick, visible in third person; rumble and phone vibration pulse with it; both on by default.
- **The state line.** No HUD. For players who cannot read snow on the shoulders or frost at the hem, the pause screen carries one line in words: *Cold. Wet to the knees. Battery about half. Three flares.* Off by default, on with larger text.
- **Hold or toggle**, independently for raise (hold on keyboard and gamepad, toggle on touch), hurry, reach out, torch (toggle). **Remappable keys** (everything but Esc and the arrows; conflicts swap). **Look**: sensitivity, invert Y, aim assist, the viewfinder factor. **Touch**: size, opacity, left-handed. **Sound**: master, pad, world; quiet sound; headphones/speakers; mute. **Performance**: render scale, adaptive quality, the copyable report.

### 8.6 Against emptiness

The risk, stated plainly by the panel: machines only, no faces, no voices, five hours, and a world that gets emptier as you go. Each counter has a number or a test.

1. **Something answers every ninety seconds.** Along the quiet bot's route the gap between events (a reply, a readable, a vantage prompt, a seat, a machine turning its head, the train, a bird) never exceeds 90 s, and on the ice 150 s, where the ice-song and the train fill it. The content behind the rule: 26 quiet encounters, 48 captioned vantage points, 31 seats with 28 lines, 4 beds, and about forty readable objects (diary pages, registry cards, the timetable, the logbook, fridge notes, the school register, the shop's last order book).
2. **The islanders are everywhere and never seen.** Every one of the ten red houses is dressed from a per-house list of the things people leave (a calendar page, a cup, foil, a child's drawing of a walker with a smile, boots by the door), and each lit house has one object the nights table takes away. Breath from every docked hull. Their Polaroids on their own walls.
3. **Roos is always one key away.** A human as murmur and text on channel one, at any moment, 4.8 s later, never asking anything, and different once after a carry. The reason the van's hazards blink on the shore from every high point.
4. **Scouts lead.** Every placed scout leads to a bench or to a bearer; 04 is in the viewfinder and in HRTF; the sled is 1.5 m behind you with its own panner; the machines turn their heads to every reply. There is no companion that follows.
5. **The place-lines return when you rest.** The loneliest verb brings the narrator's company, and the logbook writes back in a hand that is not yours.
6. **What you empty, the prints keep.** Outside the Collector's country every bearer you switch off stays where it stood, and its print shows the red light still on, with its number for a caption.
7. **Revisits change.** The epilogue trick at least four times.
8. **The night's cost is visible by looking**: the morning shot, the one morning line, the card index.
9. **The reserve**: one human figure, small and grained, at a washing line, in a single print, never live.
10. **The shape test**: the twelve-minute quiet-run rule and the ninety-second rule together are the mechanical definition of "not empty"; a build that fails them does not ship.

## 10. The charter

Sixteen rules. Each states what the player is promised, how the code keeps it, and how a test proves it. Tests live in `tools/check-charter.js` (headless, `__sim` stepping, seeded from the URL) unless another tool is named. The public lines are on the pause screen; a rule a player cannot find is only a mood.

1. **There is no clock.** No world time advances during play; light is a constant per chapter; the day counter increments only inside `sleep()` and `carry()`. *Code:* no `worldTime`; every timed behaviour is a countdown owned by an object and started by an event; a static check greps the sim for `Date.now`, `performance.now` and any frame-clock read outside the ambient layer. *Test:* at 200 sampled positions per chapter the bot stands idle 30 simulated minutes and the world-state hash is unchanged.
2. **Every countdown is yours.** Every countdown begins with a player act and ends when the act ends; the two excepted consequences are the six-second lift (which follows a flinch) and the eight-second print (a wait, not a threat). *Code:* `startCountdown(owner, cause)` requires a cause from the call enum. *Test:* a fuzzer walks each chapter making no calls and never still longer than 1 s; the countdown log is empty.
3. **Sound before sight, and the tick before the move.** Every threat emits its cue from its actual position before its first movement can be seen, by §7.8's per-type table, and the tester ticks 2.0 s before any charge-woken machine takes its first step. *Code:* a `heard` flag set by the sim-side event bus; `firstMove()` refuses to run while it is false or the tester's tick for that source is younger than 2.0 s. *Test:* `tools/timing.js` on both routes through every placed encounter.
4. **Nothing is placed behind you and nothing arrives unheard.** At chapter load and after every night no threat is within 20 m of the player's spawn or waking position, nor inside the rear cone within 30 m; a threat may cross into the rear 12 m only if it emitted a cue from its current position within the last 6 s. *Test:* placement validated at load; the timing bot asserts the 6 s rule on every rear-cone entry.
5. **Nothing hunts.** No machine closes distance on a moving player at more than a quarter of walking speed, and only a bearer that has already been in Wait follows at all; wires never leave their anchor length; walkers follow their routes; the heads stop dead when the light goes out; the Collector answers sound only, one bounded answer per call. *Test:* the bot walks every chapter continuously (torch off, no calls) and is never approached within 3 m by anything, never carried, never touched.
6. **Nothing hunts in daylight.** In `day` and `dusk` light the Collector's answer node is compiled out; it stands, and only its bell answers. Bearers still come to stillness in daylight, which is how the standoff is taught; they are answering, not hunting, and B1's first approach stops the moment the player walks. *Test:* a scripted bell in the yard under forced day light produces zero Collector steps over 10 minutes.
7. **Sit is absolute.** A sitting player cannot be approached, lifted, touched or stepped on; engaged bearers hold at 3 m; wires lower their heads; walkers are routed so no footfall lands within 4 m of a seat. Sitting freezes calm and does not remove a threat. *Test:* at 200 positions per chapter, sit, force calm to 0 and 1, enable every behaviour, step 10 minutes: no carry, no death, nothing inside 3 m; the world hash on standing equals the hash on sitting except for ambient.
8. **You can always sit down.** From any point on either bot route a seat is reachable within 90 s of walking outdoors and 45 s indoors; the sled is a seat outdoors. *Test:* a reachability pass over the route graphs at 1.4 m/s.
9. **No forced capture, no forced kill.** Every chapter is completed by a quiet bot that never raises the cutter, never lights a flare and never flinches, and by a loud bot that cuts every seam it sees; HALL 3 has four entries and none is required. *Test:* `bot-quiet` and `bot-loud` per chapter on every build.
10. **Control is never taken.** During every spectacle the player keeps every verb; the camera leaves the player's hands only after a verb the player chose: sit, sleep, read, climb, photograph, be carried, and the ending. *Test:* the bot moves during every spectacle window and its position changes; the camera-owner log shows no `cutscene` owner.
11. **No stingers, no chase, no arena, nothing louder than a footfall, and the score is deaf.** The pad and bells never read the threat system; the audio module has no import from encounters; the master ceiling is the footfall's peak. *Test:* a peak logger per bus over both bots; the pad's chord log byte-identical whether the bot is in a standoff or on open ice.
12. **Reading is rest.** Opening a diary page, a card, the timetable, the logbook or the map freezes the simulation as pause does; pending replies resume after. *Test:* open the diary with a bell reply pending; step 10 s; it has not fired; close; it fires on schedule.
13. **Failure is a night, not a reload.** Being carried spends a night and moves the world by the bounded table; real death exists only for four causes (a fall over 6 m, thin ice, a walker's footfall within its pad, contact with a wire's live anchor) and is a cold fade of 1.6 s to the last seat with no counter, no line and no lost photograph. *Code:* `die(cause)` asserts membership. *Test:* any unlisted cause fails the run; the nights test applies the table 40 times and asserts the world hash stops changing at its end.
14. **Replies never gate, never score, and come after enough silence to wonder.** Every quiet encounter's reply is 2.4 s or later, quieter than a footstep, never looped; no door, path or chapter depends on any; no reply fires once the player has left earshot. *Test:* `check-life.js`, grown from the first game.
15. **Captions carry the sound's information and no more.** Same tick as the sound, bearing and near/far, never the maker's name until that type has been seen. *Test:* `check-captions.js`.
16. **Quiet mode is the whole game.** With it on, no machine ever lifts; everything else is identical: the same places, encounters, lines, prints, album and three endings. *Test:* the quiet bot with quiet mode on and off produces identical album keys and ending flags; the carry count is zero with it on.

Pause screen: *Nothing hunts in daylight. You can always sit down. Sitting down keeps your place. Some things answer. Some things only happen if you wait.* Under quiet mode: *Bearers will watch. They will not carry you.*

## 11. Production

The plan has one organising rule: nothing past the vertical slice is built until one bearer, walking across the snow field toward a person holding still, has been judged sad and not silly. Everything before that gate exists to make the judgement possible as early as it can be made, which is week eight for a team of three. The engine milestones and their acceptance tests are §9.3; this section is the order they are taken in, the two gates, the slice, the cut order, the harness, the schedule and the risks.

### 11.1 The vertical slice

By week eight for three people (ten for two, thirteen for one with an AI pair) the following is playable end to end in one build: the van and the substation; the crossing on a slice-grade bay (a flat ice plane, no cracks, four walkers standing as static hulls on the horizon, the towers, snow, the sled on its rope); the farm with the lit window, the kitchen table, the darkroom in the cellar with the amber safelight and the eight-second print; the hole where SV-14's slab was; the porch wire; one scout; one bench; and one bearer on the snow field under chapter IV's afterglow with the house lit behind you, met by the quiet route (calm, the standoff, the touch, the minor chord) and by the loud route (a live cable, the cutter, three legs, two legs, the belly seam, the head turning, the light going off). Being carried works: six seconds, the sky, snow on the lens, waking on a slice-grade kerb under a foil blanket with a wall, a closed door and a hum behind it. M7 to M11 are not in the slice; the bay, the audio (M9a) and the darkroom are built at slice grade inside M6's weeks and finished at their own milestones after the gate.

### 11.2 The two gates

**Week three, the beam.** At dpr 1, render scale 1.0 and 24 beam steps, the target iPad holds a 33 ms p95 in the HALL 1 grey-box with the torch on, or the beam's step count goes on the ladder before anything else is built. The number is pasted from the device into `DEVELOPMENT.md`.

**Week eight, the gait.** A human judgement made once by a written procedure. Two captures of thirty seconds each: the bearer walking toward the camera on the snow field in the afterglow with the lamp coming on as it decides, and the same walk in overcast daylight. Five people who have not seen the project watch both without context and pick two words each from a list of twelve: sad, tired, patient, careful, watchful, heavy, scary, mechanical, silly, cute, comic, broken. Pass: at least four of five pick both words from the first eight, at least two of five include sad or patient, and nobody picks silly or comic first for either capture. Julian adds three yes-or-no checks of his own: it pauses before a step; it looks back; the lamp coming on reads as a decision. Secondary criteria that must also hold: the slice bots finish by both routes; the iPad holds a 33 ms p95 at the standoff with the beam on; cold boot under 6 s on the iPad; heap under 250 MB. If the gait fails, two more weeks of look-dev with the silhouette changed (lower body, longer legs, slower cadence, the head leading) and one more judging. If it fails twice, the fallback that preserves the design: bearers are only ever seen beyond 20 m in fog or at night until they are within reach, where the head and the lamp carry the shot and the gait does not; Näset's daylight bearer moves to a fog bank at the harbour. That is a real loss and it is written here so nobody pretends it is not. A pass at week eight in a slice-grade field may not hold in the finished fog and lighting; the clip is re-judged at IV's completion.

### 11.3 Chapter build order and cut order

After the gate, chapters are built in this order, each finished to the point where its bots pass and its vantage points are captioned before the next starts: II Sjögården to full (the hub, the darkroom, the slab hole); I Isen to full (the bay at M10 grade); III Näset; VI Anläggningen, begun in parallel by whoever owns `rooms.js` from week 14, because it is the longest, the darkest and the only chapter that can sink the schedule; IV Björkhagen; V Kärret; VII Isvägen (the darkroom morning, the bunk ride, the three endings); VIII Islossningen; then, only if the core is finished and playtested, Kyltornen and the Västerskär interior. The order III then IV is fixed; there is no either-order branch.

The cut order, applied from the top when the schedule slips, chosen for least damage to the argument of the game: 1 Kyltornen; 2 the Västerskär interior; 3 the state-parametric scene system beyond the four remembered frames, and hidden layers on free frames; 4 HALL 2's workshop line (five heads turning becomes one); 5 the Collector's country on the ice (it keeps the yard only, and the walk home loses its edge); 6 per-limb severing (the cutter severs only the belly seam; the graph still lights, nothing drops); 7 the walker bunk ride (walkers exterior only; VII's approach is walked); 8 the third ending, the wait (two endings, sit and walk); 9 the Västerskär crossing in Kärret (the bell is still answered from under the ice). Never cut: the crossing, the farm and the darkroom, the standoff, sit to save, HALL 3, the album, quiet mode. If the plan reaches item 9 and is still late, the game ships with six chapters and the marsh folded into the facility approach, which is the panel's four-hour cut.

### 11.4 The test harness

The harness is the spec: every rule with a number has a test, and the tests exist before the content they check. **Deterministic stepping:** `window.__sim(seconds, keys)` at the fixed step without rendering, `__manual` parking the loop, the world seeded from `?seed=`; because nothing that moves uses the RNG, two runs with the same seed and inputs produce identical bone matrices, calm traces and event logs, asserted by hashing, on Chromium only (cross-browser hashes differ in the last ulp). **The node sandbox:** `check-logic.js` loads every module in a `vm` with the arity-checking canvas Proxy and a WebGL2 stub in the same style (`uniformMatrix4fv` 3, `bufferData` 3, `texImage2D` 6 or 9, `drawElements` 4, `drawElementsInstanced` 5, finite-number checks, a draw-call record, a check that every `texSubImage2D` into the bone texture stays inside its row), and runs the logic tests: every seat has a floor under it and a shot; every vantage point is reachable on foot; every encounter has a delay and a cooldown; every board route lights a corridor that exists; every hall seat is deeper than 15 m from any portal the Collector's cable can enter; the nights table never removes a seat or a fuse; the sound table's peaks; the numbers-of-record lint over `levels.js` and `text.js`; the text lint. **Bots:** one chapter at a time by the quiet route (waypoints with a posture script) and the loud route (cut here, JORD here, flare here); a chapter passes when both finish without a real death and the quiet route kills nothing; a third run in quiet mode asserts no bearer ever lifts; a fourth on Anläggningen asserts the Collector never appears within 20 m of the player without a logged call at least 3.6 s earlier, and logs its distance after every answer. **Timing:** every delayed reply at ± 1 frame under 10 s and ± 0.25 s above (the bell, the radio, the Collector, the prompt, the approach from 8 m, KV-3, the print, the carry, the footfall, the birds, 04's return, the socket pulse). **Pixel tests:** the luminance floors per room and chapter; torch alignment within 2 percent of frame width; the colour-law lint; 04 absent from the live frame and present in the viewfinder frame of the same scene; the hidden-layer test; the painting-camera test (still 6 s in open country, the head in the lower third and sky above the horizon at least 55 percent). **Smoke and soak:** both builds, desktop and phone viewports, phone defaults on, every chapter for 1.2 s of wall time, on Chromium and on Playwright's WebKit for API paths only (Linux WebKit is not iPad Safari and says nothing about performance or rendering; `DEVELOPMENT.md` says so); thirty minutes of the bot on the ice with the heap sampled every ten seconds, failing on 5 percent growth after the fifth minute or a context loss. **iPad measurements:** the copyable report with its new fields, taken on the target iPad at M2, M3, M6, the gate, M10 and every chapter's completion, pasted into the log with the date; a number from a Mac is a number from a Mac. **The gate script** `check-all.sh` runs `check-logic`, a syntax pass, the build (failing above 3.0 MB of dist or 2.4 MB of source), then each browser suite one at a time with a thirty-minute timeout; it runs before every merge and its output is the last thing in each week's log entry.

### 11.5 Memory and boot on tablets

Budgets are §9.1's and are measured at every milestone. Site residency is hub plus ice plus one spoke, with the facility in three sub-loads (yard, halls, culverts) so no single load exceeds 400k triangles; a spoke is built by the worker when the player is within 150 m of its shore, posted as transferables, and released 300 m after leaving. ### 11.8 What not to build

No physics engine (AABBs, a capsule, verlet chains and ragdoll chunks cover doors, debris, the sled, cables and bodies; nothing stacks, nothing rolls). No animation clips and no animation tool; every motion is code on the bone tree, and the scripted moments are behaviours on the same state machine with the player free to walk. No human rig beyond the player's back; the one hooded figure, if playtests ask for it, is a decal in a developed print. No moving-room collider system: the bunk is a seat. No inventory grid, no map screen, no health bar. No dialogue system, no synthesised speech, no PA voice. No level editor: a debug fly camera and a console line that prints the Builder call for where you stand. No streaming beyond site residency. No real-time clock or day-night cycle.
