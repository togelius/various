# The fiction and the museal: Calvino, Borges and real museum practice, read for the museum roguelike

*Research brief 7 of 7, compiled 2026-10-20. It asks two questions: what makes something feel like Calvino and Borges, and what makes a game feel like a museum rather than a dungeon with paintings. Each finding ends in "→" plus the subsystems it touches. Anything I could not confirm is marked (unverified).*

**Takeaways**
1. Calvino and Borges are **formal systems**, not a mood: a series matrix, a combinatorial deck, a fake encyclopedia, a taxonomy. Borrow the structures (series, cascades, catalogues, attribution), not the furniture (mirrors, labyrinths, tigers).
2. Museum text has a published grammar: word counts, field order, attribution qualifiers, credit lines, numbering. Copied faithfully, **the tombstone label becomes the stat block and identification becomes attribution**. It is the cheapest strong "museal" signal we have.
3. Visitor studies give us pacing numbers: 4–6 objects stopped at per room, under 30 s per object, about 20 min per exhibition, and "museum fatigue" (1916).
4. The museum-as-collector is the genre's ethical fault line (colonial collections, "the faraway land", the "Chinese encyclopedia"). Handle it with **mechanics and voices** (credit lines, loans, consent), never lectures. Fred Wilson is the model.
5. Who the player is decides the ethics of looting. **Lean: a visitor who stayed past closing, with museum *roles* as classes.**

## 2. Key findings

### A. What the Calvino/Borges feel is made of

**F1. *Invisible Cities* is a matrix** (Wikipedia, "Structure").
- 55 cities in 11 named series (Memory, Desire, Signs, Thin, Trading, Eyes, Names, the Dead, the Sky, Continuous, Hidden), five each, across 9 chapters framed by Polo–Kublai dialogue.
- Series enter as **diagonal cascades**: a new series starts at #1 while older ones advance.
- Polo: "Every time I describe a city I am saying something about Venice." Kublai's atlas "preserves the differences intact" (ch. 9).

→ Departments should be **family + ordinal** ("Tides 3"): the family carries the mechanic, the ordinal the depth. An **Atlas** of families × ordinals is the meta-progression object, and Calvino's answer to oatmeal. A hidden "Venice" that every department secretly describes gives a through-line across runs. [concept, sequencing, meta]

**F2. Combinatorics needs "ghosts"** (Calvino 1967, via Aarseth).
- Literature is "a combinatorial game that pursues the possibilities implicit in its own material". It becomes meaningful when one permutation hits something that matters to the reader.
- In *Castle of Crossed Destinies*, mute travellers tell stories with tarot laid out in a grid, so adjacent stories share cards. Calvino: tarot was "a machine for constructing stories".

→ Build departments from a **shared deck** of exhibit "cards" that different departments label differently, so the same object tells two stories. Keep a human curation pass for the ghosts. [concept, text, tooling]

**F3. The Celestial Emporium knows it is arbitrary, and blames Brussels too** (Borges 1942, Weinberger trans., read in full).
- The list ("(a) those that belong to the emperor; (b) embalmed ones … (h) those that are included in this classification … (n) those that at a distance resemble flies") is credited to "Dr. Franz Kuhn" and "a certain Chinese encyclopedia".
- Borges at once adds a European institution: "The Bibliographical Institute of Brussels also exercises chaos". Its heading 179 is "Cruelty to animals. Protection of animals. Dueling and suicide from a moral point of view…". He concludes: "there is no classification of the universe that is not arbitrary and speculative."
- Wilkins's language spells meaning into words: *de* element, *deb* fire, *deba* a flame.

→ (i) "Organized in no discernible manner" should come from **the museum's own bureaucracy**, not an exoticised Other (§4.2). (ii) A **Wilkins-style code** in accession numbers and names makes generated names *learnable*. [naming, identification, sensitivities]

**F4. *The Book of Imaginary Beings* is a compiler's bestiary** (Wikipedia; Á Bao A Qu entry).
- 82 entries in 1957, 116 in 1967, 120 in 1969, to be read "at random… as one plays with the shifting patterns of a kaleidoscope".
- The A Bao A Qu (sourced to a non-existent treatise) waits on the stairs of the Tower of Victory at Chitor. It becomes more complete as a climber ascends and tumbles back to wait if the climber fails.

→ Bestiary voice = **a compiler citing sources and hearsay**. The A Bao A Qu gives a mechanic: a guardian whose completeness tracks your depth in the department and resets if you retreat. [enemies, text]

**F5. Babel: infinity is mostly noise; the value is the catalogue** (Borges 1941).
- Hexagonal galleries; books of 410 pages × 40 lines × about 80 characters over 25 symbols. Nearly every book is gibberish.
- Librarians hunt the "Crimson Hexagon" and the man who read the catalogue of catalogues.

→ A literally infinite museum is Babel. If we promise infinity, the player-facing object is **the index** (Directory, Atlas), and most of infinity can be closed doors. [concept, meta]

**F6. Tlön, Funes and Asterion are mechanics in disguise** (Borges 1940, 1942, 1947).
- **Tlön**: *hrönir* are duplicates produced by expectation, sometimes purer than originals; forgotten objects fade; artefacts leak into the real world.
- **Funes** cannot accept that the dog at 3:14 in profile is the dog at 3:15 from the front: "To think is to forget differences".
- **Asterion**, the Minotaur, narrates his house of "infinite" doors and plays at being visited by "the other Asterion".

→ A **Copies** family; **fading maps**; a **Funes rule** (identification does not generalise in this department); **first-person boss wall texts** for pathos without cutscenes; objects that leak into the next run. [enemies, items, text, meta]

**F7. Borges literally ran a museum of fake quotations.** "On Exactitude in Science" (the 1:1 map left in tatters in the western deserts) first appeared inside a piece titled "Museo" (1946), presented as a citation from a 1658 travel book. → **Fictional citations** on labels are on-genre. A **Scale** family can make the map a floor. [text, layout]

**F8. Oulipo: constraint as engine; "rats who construct the labyrinth from which they plan to escape"** (Queneau; Wikipedia).
- *Cent mille milliards*: ten sonnets cut into strips. Every line position shares rhymes, so all 10^14 combinations scan (about 200 million years of reading by Queneau's estimate; exact figure unverified).

→ The rats quote is our game in one line. Practical lesson: design **interchangeable slots with shared "rhymes"** (common label fields, room sockets, enemy roles) so any combination is well-formed. [concept, text, layout]

**F9. Perec generated a building** (Wikipedia, *Life A User's Manual*).
- A 10×10 apartment block; 99 chapters follow a knight's tour that skips one square.
- 42 lists of 10 elements are distributed with Graeco-Latin bi-squares, plus "gap" and "wrong" lists that deliberately perturb the scheme.
- *Un cabinet d'amateur* (1979) is a collector's painted gallery whose authenticity unravels.

→ A concrete **furnisher recipe**. Assign k attribute lists (material, light, enemy role, case type, label voice) across a department's rooms with orthogonal Latin squares, so no pair repeats. Then add one deliberate violation (Oulipo's *clinamen*). [placement, layout]

**F10. Piranesi and Barnum: two museums as design documents** (Clarke 2020; Millhauser 1987).
- **Piranesi**: halls named by direction and ordinal (the "Ninth Vestibule", where three tides meet once every eight years). Clouds above, sea below, no two statues alike, a journal with an index. "The Beauty of the House is immeasurable; its Kindness infinite."
- **Barnum**: a "labyrinth of passages, doors, and rooms" where a guard will lead you out on request. Three darkening underground levels and a rumoured fourth ("that way lies madness"). A Hall of Mermaids (perhaps costumed local girls), a Chamber of False Things, a gift shop, a sect living inside, staff who never leave.

→ Ordinal room naming; tides as hazards; darkening descent; helpful guards; rumoured secret levels; **residents** as NPCs. The museum **need not be evil**. [layout, NPCs, tone]

**F11. Three ways to fake knowledge** (Lem 1971; Pavić 1984; Serafini 1981).
- Lem reviews 16 non-existent books.
- Pavić writes three cross-referenced encyclopedias that contradict each other (Christian, Islamic and Hebrew sources), readable in any order.
- Serafini's encyclopedia uses an asemic script. In 2009 he said it was meant to give the feeling children have before they can read.

→ **Labels without objects** are cheap and powerful. **Three contradictory curators** per object is a format. Labels can **start illegible and become legible**, making identification a kind of literacy. [text, identification, visuals]

**F12. Le Guin's ethnography from inside, in the future tense.** *Always Coming Home* is a collage of the Kesh's own stories, recipes, maps, alphabet and music, framed by an ethnographer, Pandora, who doubts her project. The preface calls it a "possible archaeology of the future". → The model for any department about a people: **their own genres, present tense, a visibly partial curator.** [NPCs, text, sensitivities]

**F13. Eco: "The list is the origin of culture"** (Spiegel, 2009). Culture wants "to make infinity comprehensible… through catalogs, through collections in museums". → Inventory, catalogue and directory are the art form here, not UI concessions. [UI, meta]

### B. Museums and museum art

**F14. The Museum of Jurassic Technology never winks** (Wikipedia; Weschler 1995).
- Dim light, wood-and-glass vitrines, a labyrinthine plan.
- Verifiable exhibits (micro-sculptures in needle eyes, Ricky Jay's decaying dice) sit beside dubious ones (memory halls about forgetting; a bat said to fly through lead). Weschler found some invented and some true.
- It is dedicated, deadpan, to "the public appreciation of the Lower Jurassic".

→ **The single most important tone reference.** Wonder comes from blurred true/false and from apparatus, not from adjectives. [text, visuals, audio]

**F15. Typology is beautiful and loaded** (Pitt Rivers).
- Founded 1884 from about 22,000 objects; now over 600,000, "mostly gifted by colonial officials, travellers, scholars, and missionaries".
- Arranged by type across cultures to show "development from simple to complex", an evolutionist scheme kept only because of the Deed of Gift.
- In 2020 it removed the tsantsas and other human remains, which "reinforced racist and stereotypical thinking". *Labelling Matters* reviews derogatory and "Eurocentric" labels.

→ A **Classification** family is rich (cross-origin type series, synergies) but must never rank peoples. **No human remains**, invented or not. [layout, items, sensitivities]

**F16. Soane: hinged walls and a fictional resident.**
- An 1833 Act fixed the house as Soane left it.
- The Picture Room's hinged "planes" show about three times the usual number of works. Top-lit domes, convex mirrors, 90 visitors at a time, candlelit evenings.
- Soane invented a monk, "Padre Giovanni" (a pun on his own name), with a parlour, cell and sham ruined cloister, and his friends played along.

→ **Hinged walls** as the diegetic secret door. A **Founder who is a fiction inside the fiction**: our hidden Venice. [layout, NPCs, meta]

**F17. Broodthaers's fictional museum department.**
- *Musée d'Art Moderne, Département des Aigles* opened in his Brussels home in 1968 with fine-art crates and inscriptions.
- *Section des Figures* (Düsseldorf, 1972): over 300 eagle objects, each placarded "This is not a work of art".
- A Financial Section offered the museum "for sale due to bankruptcy" (Cologne, 1971) and sold eagle-stamped gold ingots at twice the gold price.

→ Precedent for **"a department about one arbitrary thing"** and for the institution's own machinery (crates, sales, bankruptcy) as content. [concept, text]

**F18. Fred Wilson: arrangement and labels are arguments** (*Mining the Museum*, 1992–93).
- Using only the Society's own holdings, he put slave shackles in "Metalwork 1793–1880" among silver, and chairs facing a whipping post under "Cabinet Making".
- He placed empty pedestals for Douglass, Banneker and Tubman beside busts of Napoleon, Clay and Jackson.
- Reviewers: "suggestive rather than didactic, provocative rather than moralizing". The whipping post had in fact punished wife-beaters; Wilson said it was "charged by what you bring to it".

→ **Meaning by juxtaposition, credit line and absence**, never sermon. Also a mechanic: **re-hanging** objects between cases changes their effect. [sensitivities, items, text]

**F19. Fiction is established museum practice.** Raad's *Atlas Group* is a fictional archive with fictional donors. Kabakov labels a fictional man's rubbish. The *Lambshead Cabinet* invents a collector's objects. Pamuk's novel chapters correspond to museum vitrines. Dion classified Thames beachcombing into a cabinet. The Met's 2021 Afrofuturist Period Room "acknowledges the fiction of every period room".

→ Generated donors, collectors and period rooms are on solid ground. Period rooms are the hand-authored set-piece slot. [text, layout]

**F20. Absence is a display technique.**
- The Gardner still hangs empty frames from its 1990 theft (13 works, including Vermeer's *The Concert*).
- AMNH closed two halls in Jan 2024 rather than show objects without tribal consent.
- The Horniman transferred 72 Benin objects to Nigeria in 2022 and now displays some *on loan from* Nigeria's NCMM.

→ **Empty frames, closed galleries and changed credit lines are complete stories.** [layout, items, sensitivities]

### C. Museum conventions, practically

**F21. Label hierarchies have published word budgets** (V&A 2013 [T]; BM 2022 [T]).
- **V&A**: intro panel 150–180 words; section panel 130–150; object label 50–60; group label 70. First sentence ideally under 16 words.
- **V&A ten points** include "admit uncertainty", "bring in the human element" and "write as you would speak". It cites the Getty "art novice", who spends "less than 30 seconds looking at an object".
- **BM**: title panel about 50; section about 100; **gateway** label about 120 (an outstanding object that introduces its section); wall quotations at most 25; standard labels 60; group labels 80.
- **BM field order**: description; origin, culture, date; provenance; material; commentary; donor or lender; department abbreviation and registration number.

→ A complete **text schema with length limits**, ready for a generator and validator. [text, tooling, testing]

**F22. Visitors attend to little, measurably** (BM 2022; Serrell 2016).
- BM permanent galleries: a 2 h 20 min visit; visitors stop at "about four to six objects in any given room", spending 3–4 min per room.
- Serrell's database of 110 exhibitions: average visit "20 minutes". The "thoroughly used" benchmark (over 51% of visitors stopping at more than half the elements) was met by only 7 of 51 studies in 2009. Small exhibitions are used more fully.

→ Each room needs **4–6 attention-worthy things, one of them a gateway**. A department should take about 15–25 minutes. Most labels will go unread, so every mechanical truth also needs an **icon**. [layout, placement, text, difficulty]

**F23. Museum fatigue is real** (Gilman 1916; Bitgood).
- Gilman photographed visitors contorting themselves at cases.
- Later work separates physical fatigue, mental fatigue, **satiation** (similar objects in a row) and competition between objects.
- Cures: seating, café and shops, variety, lower density, a choice of route.

→ A diegetic hunger analogue comes with its own cures. **Vary within rooms**, not only across departments. [difficulty, layout]

**F24. Attribution qualifiers are a confidence scale** (ARLIS/NA–VRA [T]).
- *Attributed to* means "uncertainty".
- *Workshop of* means assistants, probably supervised.
- *Circle of* means a distinct, unidentified associated hand.
- *Follower of* "may not actually be contemporary".
- *Style/Manner of* means the master "had little or nothing to do" with it.
- *After* means a copy.

→ An **identification ladder** that reads as both museum and RPG. "After X" is a weaker copy. [items, identification]

**F25. Numbers, credit lines and rooms carry history.**
- Met accession numbers encode year, lot and item: 29.100.23 = "H.O. Havemeyer Collection, Bequest of Mrs. H.O. Havemeyer, 1929".
- BM registration numbers run year, month-day, item (e.g. 1802,0710.1 for the Rosetta Stone, "EA 24"; my recollection, unverified).
- Credit lines distinguish gift, bequest, purchase, transfer and loan.
- Rooms are fixed addresses: the BM's Room 4 holds the Rosetta Stone; Met Gallery 131 is Dendur, audio stop 1140.

→ **Accession number = seed and ID; credit line = item state; room numbers = coordinates**, and a gap in the numbering is a secret. [items, layout, meta]

**F26. Light follows material** (Texas Historical Commission 2013). The maximum is 50 lux for textiles, paper and photographs, and 150 lux for oils, wood and leather; stone and metal tolerate more. The Met's Luce Center keeps light-sensitive items out of constant view. → **Room lux comes from the material on display**: paper rooms are dark and good for stealth; stone halls are bright. [layout, visuals]

**F27. Museums are ritual scripts and watching machines.**
- Duncan treats the museum "like a script or score" that the visitor performs.
- Bennett's "exhibitionary complex" joins spectacle and mutual watching.
- Foucault: museums are heterotopias that accumulate time indefinitely.
- Greenblatt separates **resonance** (an object reaching out to its culture) from **wonder** (an object that stops you with "exalted attention").

→ The **official tour** as a scripted path that rewards compliance. A **being-watched** system. License for any period to sit beside any other. A **wonder/resonance dial** to alternate across a run against satiation. [player choice, NPCs, sequencing]

## 3. Ideas for the museum roguelike

### 3.1 The museal grammar

| Convention | Game element | Subsystem |
|---|---|---|
| **Tombstone label** (F21, F25) | **The stat card.** Maker/culture = department and faction. Date = tier ("about", "probably" while unidentified). Medium = damage type and resistances. Dimensions = size and weight. Credit line = state. Number = seed and ID. | items, enemies, UI |
| **Didactic label** (50–90 words) | Flavour that must contain **exactly one true mechanical hint**, phrased as observation ("keepers report…"), checked by a validator against mechanic tags. | text, testing |
| **Section panel** | A rule announced when entering a zone ("In this section the tide reaches the second step"). | text, layout |
| **Intro panel** | Department card: family, the Big Idea, the signature mechanic in one sentence. | concept, text |
| **Gateway object** (BM) | One per section: mini-boss, key item or set-piece. | enemies, placement |
| **Wall quotation** (≤25 words) | Voice of a culture, NPC or boss (Asterion). | text, NPCs |
| **Attribution ladder** (F24) | Identification tiers: Manner of → Follower of → Attributed to → named. Re-attribution can *downgrade*. Family labels double as an assist mode. | items, difficulty |
| **Wilkins-coded number** (F3) | `W.4.212.3` = family letter · department · room lot · item. Players learn to read partial identity. Shareable seed. | items, tooling |
| **Credit lines** | *Gift of*: clean. *Purchase, … Fund*: shop. *Bequest of the visitor who fell in Room 512*: your previous death. *Transferred from*: family synergy. *On loan from*: strong, must be returned or a Claim follows. *Found in the collection, circumstances unrecorded*: risky. | items, meta, sensitivities |
| **Condition report** | Durability graded Excellent / Good / Fair / Poor; the Conservator repairs. | items |
| **"Not on view" / "On loan elsewhere"** | A label without its object: hints at secrets and future departments (Lem). | text |
| **"Closed for reinstallation"** | Locks, scaffolding, shortcuts. | layout |
| **"Closed: consultation in progress"** (F20) | A room in a Peoples department closed at their request. No loot, no lecture. | sensitivities |
| **Room numbers** (F25), ordinals (F10) | Department d uses rooms d00–d99. A **missing number = secret room**. | layout, UI |
| **Floor plan; audio-guide stops** | The map is an Information Desk item (sometimes outdated). The audio guide is an equippable that narrates enemy intent; unlisted stop numbers form a hidden tour. | UI, audio |
| **Docents and tours** (F27) | Join a tour to pass guards, as Konigsburg's runaways hid among school groups. | NPCs, choice |
| **Guards and posts** (Bringley; Barnum) | A neutral faction rotating between posts, hostile if you touch exhibits. A guard will escort you out for a fee (retire the run). | NPCs, stealth |
| **Vitrines, plinths, ropes** | Vitrines: see-through, impassable, breakable (alarm). Plinths: raised tiles with dormant statues. Ropes: passable but noisy. | layout |
| **Lux by material** (F26) | Room light sets vision radius; carrying the lantern is a choice. | layout, visuals |
| **Dioramas, period rooms** | Glass-fronted niches whose inhabitants are released if the glass breaks. Period rooms are authored vignettes. | enemies, layout |
| **Open storage / "Order an Object"** | Unlabelled shelving maze (unidentified loot); a kiosk orders one specific item for a fee. | items |
| **Gift shop, cloakroom, café, benches** | Shop placed before the exit. **Cloakroom: one checked item survives death.** Café and benches heal and relieve fatigue. | economy, meta |
| **Museum fatigue** (F23) | Optional "Attention" pool spent on reading and identifying; drained by satiation; refilled by benches and variety. | difficulty |
| **Conservation lab, Registrar** | Conservation: repair and uncurse. Registrar: identify, and **accession** one item permanently. | NPCs, meta |
| **Membership, donor walls, Lost Property** | Meta tiers (Friend → Benefactor); a gallery named after your best run; items from your last death reappear at Lost Property. | meta |
| **Temporary exhibitions** (F22) | Ticketed, harder, narrative-dense optional departments or daily seeds. | sequencing |

(The closing-time clock is developed in the sibling briefs; the museal version is the announcement, then dimmed lights, then the night shift.)

### 3.2 Department families: Calvino's series with mechanical motifs

Departments are named "Family + ordinal" (e.g. *Tides 2*). A first build needs about six of the twelve: Copies, Light, Tides, Machines, Specimens, Classification.

| Family | Mechanical motif | Flavour |
|---|---|---|
| **Copies** | Everything in multiples with one original per room. Breaking the original weakens its copies, and copies of copies degrade. | Forgers, doubles; a hrön boss purer than its original. |
| **Memory** | Unrevisited rooms fade from the map. Funes rule. Forgotten enemies lose you. | Souvenirs, mnemonic devices. |
| **Scale** | Step into a model to play a sub-room; a 1:1 map floor reveals the department; giant exhibits are terrain. | Tiny swarms, map-wraiths, lenses. |
| **Classification** | A department taxonomy drives damage and aggro: enemies in one arbitrary class share alertness. Sorting puzzles open doors. | Cross-origin type series; no ranking of peoples. |
| **Light** | Light as resource and threat: photosensitive enemies; your lantern attracts and repels. | Lectern moths (sample 2). |
| **Absence** | Invisible enemies inferred from labels, dust and sound. **Exhibit** an item on an empty plinth for an aura. | Frames that hold what you put in them. |
| **Peoples** (§4.2) | A living community; three-voice labels; loans, consent, reputation and trade instead of default hostility. | Lenders, keepers (sample 4). |
| **Machines** | Deterministic mechanisms, hourly "demonstrations", automata with exact readable patterns. | Escapement hounds (sample 1), devices with charges (sample 6). |
| **Tides** | Periodic floods and storms; each period room locked to its own hour. | Weather in jars, umbrellas (sample 3). |
| **Specimens** | Dormant creatures behind glass. A Bao A Qu pattern: completeness tracks your progress. | A compiler-voiced bestiary. |
| **Writing** | Asemic labels become legible as you learn the script; inscriptions act as spells. | Indexers, purifiers. |
| **Attendance** | You are the exhibit: a visibility meter, rewards for following the tour, past deaths on display. | Attendants, followers (sample 5). |

The sources for each family are in F3–F20 (e.g. Copies ← Tlön, "After", Perec's *Cabinet*; Tides ← Piranesi; Attendance ← Bennett, Duncan, Broodthaers).

**Naming, Celestial Emporium style.** A slot grammar whose slots carry the family:
- *[Things] That [odd verb]*: "Instruments That Keep Time Badly"
- *[Noun], Considered as [Noun]*: "Doors, Considered as Animals"
- *[Things] Known Only From [evidence]*: "Machines Known Only From Their Repair Manuals"
- *[Things] Lent by [improbable lender]*
- *[Things] Not Yet [participle]*
- Rare self-reference: "Departments Currently Closed"

The **lobby Directory** is the one place for open homage:

> (a) Instruments that belong to the weather; (b) doors, considered as animals; (c) things lent by their own shadows; (d) clocks that disagree; (e) copies better than their originals; (f) machines not yet invented; (g) animals described by witnesses who did not see them; (h) departments currently closed; (i) weathers, domesticated; (j) objects never unpacked; (k) furniture for rooms that no longer exist; (l) et cetera (storage); (m) things that have just moved; (n) things that, from the far end of the gallery, resemble visitors.

None of these is a people. The absurdity belongs to the institution (F3).

**Sequencing**
- Doors carry absurd shared attributes ("Continue to Rooms 500–540: Things That Hum") that preview the next family.
- No family appears twice in a row, and wonder and resonance alternate.
- The **Atlas** grid unlocks higher ordinals across runs.
- A **hidden Venice**: one recurring object (an umbrella, a key) is labelled differently in every department. Collecting its readings opens the unlisted Founder's cabinet (Soane's monk).

### 3.3 Voice and tone guide

The register is **institutional deadpan** (F14): precise, a little tired, occasionally kind (Piranesi), never whimsical about itself.

1. **House rules** (F21): first sentence under 16 words, about something visible; 50–90 words (gateway up to 120); active verbs; answer the obvious question.
2. **Specific beats grand**: one material detail, one repair, one measurement.
3. **Admit uncertainty**: "probably", "about", "attributed to", "keepers report". This is diegetic identification, not vagueness.
4. **Exactly one mechanical truth**, generated from tags and mirrored by an icon.
5. **A human presence**: a maker, lender, keeper or guard.
6. **Plural voices** for anything cultural: curator text, a community quotation (≤25 words), sometimes dissent.
7. **Banned**: labyrinth, mirror (as theme), infinite, tiger, dream, Babel, eldritch, arcane, ancient, mysterious, magical; exclamation marks; jokes at the makers' expense. *The museum is the genre, not Borges.*
8. **Invented calendars** are anchored to institutional events ("after the Second Calibration"). Never real centuries or places.
9. **Prefer role-names** ("the Keeper of the Ledger, eleventh of that title") over personal names that read as a real language.

**Six sample labels.** The italic line after each is the mechanic and is not shown to the player.

**Escapement Hound**
Workshop of the Guild of Regulators, Lower Horology · Probably made after the Second Calibration · Brass, gut string, lacquered walnut; one tooth, not original · H. 61 cm, L. 94 cm. Weight not recorded (the animal declined the scales) · Transferred from the Department of Instruments That Keep Time Badly · M.7.212.4

Households that could not afford a clock could sometimes afford a hound. This one walked its owners' rooms on the beat of its own escapement, the small wheel you can see turning through the open panel in its flank. Between beats it is entirely still. The Regulators' manual advises speaking to it "on the off-beat". The left foreleg is a later repair in paler brass. Keepers report that it ignores anything that does not tick, and follows anything that does.

*Acts every second turn, moving two tiles; aggro only toward ticking items and noise; weak on the left.*

**Lectern Moth** (mounted specimen, with larval galleries)
Pest collection, Department of Works on Paper · Collected during the Great Reshelving · Specimen pinned to card; tunnels bored through a printed catalogue, pp. 112–140 · Wingspan 11 cm · Conservation Department study collection · C.P.0091

This is why the lights in this department are kept so low. The lectern moth eats printed matter and prefers labels to books, perhaps because labels are better lit. Its larvae tunnel exactly along the lines of type, so a damaged catalogue can still be read against a lamp: the missing words are the ones that were eaten. Adults fly towards any light brighter than fifty lux. Please lower your lantern.

*Swarms toward light; destroys unread scrolls; lowering the lantern hides you; damaged scrolls reveal effects by negation.*

In the upper halls, where the weather is part of the collection, umbrellas were handed out with the admission ticket and collected at the exit. This one was never collected. **Ledger of Debts Forgiven**
Kept by the Porters' Hall of the Ninety Landings; entries in many hands · Begun before the Fourth Flood and still in use · Rag paper, waxed thread, iron-gall ink; page tabs of dyed fish-skin · H. 31 cm, W. 22 cm, D. 9 cm (thicker each time it is lent) · Lent by the Porters' Hall, who ask that it be displayed open · Loan L.338.1

Most ledgers record what is owed. This one records only what was let go: each line is a debt struck out, signed by the person who struck it. The Porters lend it one page at a time and collect it when the page is full. "We do not show the debts. We show that we carried them and put them down." *Once per run it forgives shop debt. It is a loan: taking it closes the Porters' rooms and raises a Claim; leaving it builds reputation.*

### 3.4 Who is the player?

| Option | Precedent | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Visitor past closing** | Konigsburg's runaways in the Met; *Night at the Museum* | An everyman; "make it out" is natural; death = "found in the morning, escorted out". | Needs a reason to fight: things wake after hours (Barnum). |
| **B. Night guard** | Bringley; Barnum's staff | Keys as progression; containment justifies combat. | A guardian who loots is incoherent; "escape" is odd for staff. |
| **C. Thief** | Gardner heist | Stealth and alarms are native. | Turns collecting into theft; flirts with the colonial loop unless reframed as stealing *back*. |
| **D. Lost child** | Konigsburg | Wonder and vulnerability. | Child melee is awkward. |
| **E. Copyist** | Licensed copyists in museums (unverified details) | A **sketchbook** that learns abilities from exhibits; "After X" items are your copies. | Low urgency on its own. |
| **F. Escaped exhibit** | Wilson's turned gaze; sample 5 | Inverts the gaze; death = re-accession. | Must not read as a people escaping. |
| **G. Courier** | Loan couriers; Horniman | Each run carries one object home to its lenders: repatriation as the objective, without a lecture. | Escort constraints. |

**Lean: A as the default, with roles as classes.** Visitor first; Copyist and Guard as early unlocks; Thief, Courier and Escaped Exhibit later. In a museum, a class *is* a way of being in the building, and having several roles gives the looting question several angles.

**Small extras**
- **Death becomes an exhibit**: "Figure of a Visitor (Run 14). Bequest of the Night Staff", with your build in the tombstone.
- **Hinged Picture Room walls** as the standard secret door.
- **A resident NPC** (Barnum's sect, Soane's monk) who trades gossip and unlisted audio stops.
- **A small pool of invented authorities** whose citations recur across departments and are sometimes wrong.

## 4. Pitfalls and sensitivities

### 4.1 Pitfalls

1. **Borges oatmeal.** Mirrors, labyrinths and "infinite" in every label is pastiche, and it is where LLM text converges first. Use the banned list and borrow structures, not motifs.
2. **Whimsy creep.** Winking labels destroy the MJT effect and make every department the same theme park.
3. **Text overload** (F22). Tombstone always; didactic on inspect; mechanics as icons; never gate progress on a long label.
4. **Satiation** (F23). Variety must exist inside rooms (Perec squares).
5. **Babel** (F5). Infinity without a curator and a through-line is noise.
6. **A static museum.** Use the after-hours premise, dormant creatures and the closing clock.
7. **Lying labels.** Uncertainty must be declared ("attributed to"). Unreliability belongs in a named module (a disgraced registrar), never in accidents.
8. **Impersonation.** Never name real museums, curators or donors in generated text.

### 4.2 Sensitivities

**The problem.**
- Universal museums were filled largely through empire. Pitt Rivers objects are "mostly gifted by colonial officials… and missionaries". Sarr & Savoy cite the estimate that over 90% of sub-Saharan Africa's material heritage is outside the continent. Hicks documents the 1897 Benin loot.
- A loop of *enter the faraway department, kill its inhabitants, take its objects* reproduces that history.
- The literary models carry the same baggage. Calvino's frame is Marco Polo reporting to an Asian emperor. Borges projects absurdity onto "a certain Chinese encyclopedia", which critics such as Zhang Longxi (1988) read as China serving as the West's Other (argument unverified here). The brief's "faraway land" is the trope in miniature.

**Rules that are thoughtful without lecturing**
1. **Most departments are not about peoples.** Like Calvino's cities, each is an idea (a phenomenon, technology, creature or institution). Cap Peoples at about 1 in 4.
2. **No real-culture signifiers as shorthand.** No robe-and-desert people, mask tribes, pseudo-samurai or pyramid civilisations, and no invented languages that mimic a real one's phonology. Generate cultures from a *conceit* ("a people who record only debts forgiven"), and run a review pass asking "which real group does this read as?".
3. **Absurdity belongs to the institution** (Borges's own Brussels pairing, F3).
4. **No human remains**, invented or not (Pitt Rivers 2020). A sign reading "The Museum no longer displays the dead" is a museal beat.
5. **No sacred objects used against their makers' wishes** without the lenders reacting.
6. **Voice and agency** (Le Guin): their own genres, present tense, and the power to lend, refuse and close rooms.
7. **Provenance as mechanic, not sermon** (Wilson, Horniman):
   - An item "Found in the collection, circumstances unrecorded" is strong but carries a Claim.
   - Returning it changes the credit line to "Lent by…". It trades raw power for a relation (an ability, reputation, a shortcut), and over a run that is often the better deal. That trade is the whole argument.
   - The Courier class makes the journey home the objective.
   - "Closed: consultation in progress" rooms explain themselves.
8. **No moralising text.** Put a community quotation beside the curator's text and let the gap speak: "suggestive rather than didactic" (F18).

## 5. Decisions for the design discussion

**D1. Who is the player?** (a) Visitor after closing; (b) guard; (c) thief; (d) visitor by default, with museum roles as classes. *Lean (d):* it makes classes diegetic and treats looting from several angles.

**D2. Register of the institution.** (a) Deadpan-sincere (MJT); (b) whimsical; (c) melancholic-benevolent (Piranesi); (d) satirical (Broodthaers). *Lean (a) with an undertow of (c).* Satire only at the museum's own machinery, never at invented peoples.

**D3. Text budget.** (a) Tombstone only; (b) tombstone, plus a didactic on inspect, plus section panels; (c) the full BM hierarchy. *Lean (b) plus one intro panel per department*, with V&A/BM caps enforced by a validator and every mechanical truth also shown as an icon.

**D4. Identification.** (a) Pixel Dungeon-style appearances; (b) attribution ladder; (c) script legibility; (d) (b) + (c), plus Wilkins-coded numbers. *Lean (d)*, with the Funes rule as an occasional modifier.

**D5. Sequencing.** (a) Random; (b) branching doors with absurd shared attributes; (c) (b), plus the Atlas matrix across runs, plus the hidden Venice. *Lean (c).*

**D6. Peoples.** (a) None; (b) rare (≤1 in 4) with voice, loans and consent; (c) common. *Lean (b).* (a) loses Le Guin and provenance; (c) is risky at generator scale.

**D7. Provenance and repatriation.** (a) Absent; (b) flavour only; (c) light mechanic (credit-line state, Claims, return-for-relation); (d) central, via the Courier class. *Lean (c) as the baseline, (d) as an unlockable class.*

**D8. Families at launch.** (a) 6; (b) 12; (c) open-ended, proposed by an LLM. *Lean (a)*: Copies, Light, Tides, Machines, Specimens, Classification. Add Peoples only after a sensitivity review process exists. Families stay authored even if departments are generated.

**D9. Meta framing.** (a) Membership tiers or a skill store; (b) cloakroom (keep one item); (c) death becomes an exhibit, plus Lost Property; (d) the Atlas grid. *Lean: layer all four.* (b) and (d) are the core, (a) is the store, (c) is the emotional hook.

**D10. Is the museum the antagonist?** (a) Hostile (Barnum's fourth level); (b) indifferent bureaucracy; (c) benevolent but doing its job, which includes collecting *you*. *Lean (c):* humane, and it makes escape bittersweet.
